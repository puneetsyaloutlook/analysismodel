    Source: attached text of a Medium article, https://medium.com/p/86c8e7b6c7c9/edit (page could not be fetched; text supplied by the user). Title: Adopt an AI, today: a workaround for measuring AI transformation. Date and publisher not given.

    Adopt an AI, today: a workaround for measuring AI transformation A big part of the AI economic chain depends on enterprises getting value from the AI-based solutions they deploy. What does value mean to these enterprises? Boards mostly point AI at cost reduction and customer service, and executives report productivity gains. Neither shows up quickly in financial results, and few organisations can trace either back to AI specifically. So AI programs report adoption instead, because it can be counted from the first week of a rollout. Following their lead, vendors set their pricing based on seats and usage, and consultancies build their frameworks around it. Adoption seems to have gone mainstream as the way organisations describe their progress with AI, and rollouts are run like adoption drives. Why is adoption the easy go-to for AI transformation? For one, it counts use: how many people use an app or agent, how often they use its AI features, and how many tokens they consume. That use is then reported as adoption of AI. For two, it is read as a stand-in for headcount. High usage suggests the same work could be done by fewer people, which gives adoption a financial meaning, whether or not the link is real. The problems are just as obvious: Use and effective work can move in opposite directions. A person who needs ten attempts and then finishes the job by hand consumes more tokens than a person who gets a usable result the first time. A team that runs the old process alongside the new one, maybe because it does not trust the new one, still counts as fully adopted. Usage figures also cannot show what makes an app or agent useful. That depends on much more than its AI component: whether it fits the task, how it connects to the other tools in the workflow, how good its data is, and whether people trust what it produces. Nor do the figures show whether the work is done better, faster or more reliably, which is what the original investment was for. The largest gap is value that adoption cannot see at all. When AI speeds up the steps of an existing process, the gain can be measured, because the same work now takes less time and can be compared with last year's figures. But some of the most valuable changes do not come from making existing work faster. They remove the need for it. If a customer's documents update automatically when their circumstances change, nobody has to request them, process the request or answer the follow-up call. If a product is built so that a common error cannot happen, the team that checked for that error has nothing left to check. AI may be part of how either change works, but nothing in it is being used more than it was before, in fact there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with. An adoption measure has no way to record this value. These gaps come from the basic concept that adoption measures are built on. AI adoption assumes that people actually "adopt AI". Taken literally, they do not. People use tools and learn new jobs AI is not a single thing that a person chooses and takes responsibility for. In enterprise use, it is one element of the tools people use at work, and what people do with those tools falls into two cases. In the first case, a person uses a tool that has some AI built into it. An email program suggests replies, a CRM ranks sales leads, and a document editor drafts a first version. The person is using their email, their CRM or their editor, and may not know which parts of it use AI. In the second case, a person learns a new job because some of the work is now done by agents. Someone who used to process supplier invoices now reviews the invoices an agent could not match and decides what happens to them. That person is learning a job, with new tasks, judgements and accountabilities. Neither case is adopting AI. Nobody describes booking a flight online as adopting ecommerce, or describes a new project management app as adopting the cloud. Web search followed this path. In the late 1990s, finding something online meant learning query syntax with operators and quotation marks. Now people type what they want into a search box, and nobody talks about adopting search. AI features are being built into email, documents, CRMs and search itself, and they are becoming ordinary parts of the tools people use for their work. A program built around adopting AI is organised around a technology that is becoming a component of everything. Adoption tries to measure both cases, but only by counting use. For a tool, what matters is not how many people use it but how: as it was designed, or through a workaround. And for a new type of job, what matters is whether the job supports people's expertise, and whether the job is designed well enough to produce the results it was created for. Usage figures answer neither question. People use a tool as designed or work around it Faced with any tool at work, a person does one of two things. They use it as designed, or they find a workaround that gets the job done. If adoption means anything at work, it means the tool works well enough, as designed, for people to get the job done with it. A workaround is its opposite. A workaround does not show up as non-use. The person still has the licence, still opens the tool and still counts as an active user, so on paper the tool has found a home. The work is done another way, and the usage figures cannot show the difference. Workarounds happen at every level, from the interface to team leadership Interface. The tool does not show everything a person needs to complete the task in one place, so they keep other applications open beside it and piece the information together themselves. Or the approved tool is slow, so people use a personal one instead. Task. An agent sends certain cases to a person for review. Staff who see the review as unnecessary change details in the underlying records so that the case no longer triggers it, and the agent completes the work without them. Workflow. An automated step passes work to a person without the context they need, so they rebuild it from other tools. Or each automated step delivers its results somewhere different, so the person has to go looking for their work across several applications instead of having it arrive in one queue. Team. It is the busiest time of year and nobody has had time to do the training, so the team agrees to open the tool each day so that their usage is recorded, then carry on working the old way. Leadership. A manager expects strong adoption figures to be used as an argument for a smaller team. They make sure the team records enough usage to stay compliant, but not so much that it suggests the same work could be done with fewer people.

    These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation. Each workaround shows where the design failed People build workarounds because they are responsible for getting the work done and the tool as designed did not let them do it. Each workaround shows which part of the design failed, for whom, and what people do instead. Treating it as resistance discards that information. Training people to use the tool as designed will not help when the design is the reason they work around it. When an adoption does not work out, the common explanations are that staff are resisting, that leaders are not engaged enough, or that the organisation did not follow the program. Each explanation attributes the failure to people. None of them questions the measures the program used or the design of the tools it introduced. A program measured by adoption rates cannot see workarounds, so when the work does not improve it has no evidence about the design and looks for the cause in people instead. The fix has to be made at the level where the workaround happens: Interface workarounds need usable, effective interfaces: bringing together everything a task needs in one view, and acceptable speed. Task workarounds need checks that ask for real judgement, not approval. A review should show the person the specific decision the agent could not make and what depends on it, and should happen only where that judgement can change the outcome. Workflow workarounds need the workflow redesigned: which steps people do and which steps are automated, what context passes at each handover and where the work arrives, and how people recover when an automated step gets it wrong. I call this workflow experience, or WX. Team workarounds need time to learn the new way of working built into the team's workload, such as training scheduled outside peak periods, and a named person accountable for the workflow's outcomes. Leadership workarounds need measures based on the outcomes of the work rather than on usage, and a clear statement of how adoption figures will and will not be used, so that reporting honestly does not put the team at risk.

    What an AI program should measure and do instead AI is changing work in ways that are hard to picture in advance. Few people, in any role, have a reliable way to think through what a tool with AI in it will do to a job, a team or a service. "AI adoption" gives organisations a familiar way to think about it, borrowed from earlier software rollouts, where success meant getting people to use the new system. In that sense, adoption is itself a workaround: a way of managing the immediate situation until a clearer picture emerges of what living with and caring for your AI involves. A program still needs early evidence, because the value of AI takes time to show up in the numbers. Instead of a single usage figure, it can report value in tiers, from what can be shown in the first weeks to the end state the program is working towards: Short term: do the tools work as designed? Observe the work as it is done, ask people what they do when a tool does not give them what they need, and record each workaround by level as a design issue with an owner. Workarounds found and removed can be reported from the first weeks of a rollout, which is when adoption figures are reported now. Medium term: is the work getting better? Once the changes have settled, track time, quality, errors, and the experience of staff and customers for each piece of work the program changes, compared with how it was done before. Longer term: can people do the new jobs? As roles change, check that each new job, designed alongside the tools it works with, produces the results it was created for. End state: is the program reaching the future it set out to reach? From the start, write a concrete picture of how the redesigned work will be done, who or what does each part, and what the organisation and its customers gain. Test it through small trials, and report progress towards it, including work that is no longer needed.

    One look at that list, and it's obvious: none of these tiers is new. Each comes from established practice in building valuable solutions: observe how the work is really done, design for the result rather than the tool, make sure people can do what is asked of them, and start from a clear picture of what is being built. These principles were true before AI and are still true now. Organisations are having to change fast, and may well feel a bit stressed or helpless. It's worth remembering that we've done this before with methods that still work, even when we cannot yet picture how bringing the AI home will eventually turn out.
