# Sorting the article's claims and measures by the DeLone and McLean dimensions

Reference structure: DeLone and McLean (2003), as described in E4. The six dimensions are information quality, system quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction and net benefits. The article has no item IDs, so the IDs below (M for adoption measures, C for claims, W for workaround examples, F for proposed fixes, T for proposed measure tiers) are mine. Every quotation was checked by code against the article text.

## Step 1 and 2: items and lens

I listed 38 items: the measures, claims, workaround examples, fixes and measure tiers that the article states. The lens is the six dimensions in E4. Because the article discusses a measurement problem, I added one holding category, "Outside the lens", for statements about the article's own evidence or method that describe none of the six dimensions.

## Step 3: category definitions

- Information quality: The quality of the information a system produces or holds, such as accuracy, completeness and context.
- System quality: The quality of the tool or system itself as designed, such as usability, fit to the task, speed and integration.
- Service quality: The quality of the support users receive, such as training, help and time to learn.
- Use: How much and how often the system is used, including intention to use. E4 states that use is separate from net benefits.
- User satisfaction: Users' satisfaction with the system, including trust and attitudes towards it.
- Net benefits: The effect of the system on individuals, work, organisations or customers, such as time, quality, cost and headcount.
- Outside the lens: Statements about the article's own evidence or method that fit none of the six dimensions.

## Step 6: reading the spread

**Where the items cluster.** Use holds 16 of 38 items (42%) and System quality holds 9 (24%). Net benefits holds 8 (21%). The cluster in Use is expected: the article's subject is the adoption measure, and all three measures it names as adoption measures (M1 to M3) are Use. The cluster in System quality comes from the workaround examples (W1 to W3), the fixes (F1 to F3) and the short-term tier (T1).

**Categories with few or no entries.**
- Information quality has no primary entries. It appears only as a second category for C8, W3 and F3, where the article mentions "how good its data is" and the "context they need" at a handover. The article names no measure, claim or fix about the quality of information as a cause of workarounds or of value.
- User satisfaction has one primary entry (C19), and that item is about how failures get explained, not about a satisfaction measure. User satisfaction appears as a second category four times (C7, C8, W2, T2). The only proposed measure that touches it is "the experience of staff and customers" inside the medium-term tier.
- Service quality has two entries (W4, F4), both about time to learn and training.

**Net benefits: what kind of items are there.** The 8 Net benefits items are: two expectations that boards and executives hold (C1, C2), three statements about what usage figures cannot show (C9, C10 and the leadership fix F5), and three proposed measure tiers (T2, T3, T4). None reports an observed net benefit. In the material, net benefits appear as an expectation, as something adoption cannot see, or as a future measure. The material holds no example in which an organisation, a team or a customer is shown to have gained or lost from a tool.

**Evidence for the claims.** Across all 38 items the material gives no figures, no named organisations, no named studies and no counts. The one statement about evidence (C17, "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation.") comes after the five workaround levels, gives no source, and cannot be tied to a specific study. The material cannot show whether the workaround examples (W1 to W5) were observed or are illustrations. The article's own reasoning about use is therefore supported by argument, not by data.

## Does the article diagnose the phenomenon accurately?

**The part the lens supports.** The article treats use as one thing and net benefits as another, and argues that counts of use cannot show how a tool is used (C14, C15, C20) or what value results (C9, C10, C11). E4 states that use is a separate dimension from net benefits, so the article's central logical move matches the reference structure. A count of use, by its definition, cannot tell a designed use from a workaround. That part of the diagnosis is sound as reasoning.

**The part the lens does not support.** The first half of the diagnosis is that organisations do measure AI transformation by adoption (C3, C4, M1 to M3). The material gives no evidence of how many organisations do this, or in which sectors. C3 and C4 are plain assertions. The claim that adoption is "read as a stand-in for headcount" (C5) is qualified by the article itself ("whether or not the link is real"), which is honest, but the frequency of the practice is unknown.

**Claims about value.** The strongest claim about missed value is C10, that some changes "remove the need for" work. The two illustrations (automatic document updates, error-proof product design) are hypothetical and carry no source. The reasoning is valid, since a removed task has no baseline to compare, but its importance ("the largest gap") is asserted, not measured.

**Analogies.** C12 and C13 argue that "adopting AI" is a category error by comparison with ecommerce, cloud and search. The comparison shows how language changes as a technology becomes ordinary. It does not show that adoption measures fail now. This claim rests on analogy alone.

## What the article assumes and leaves out, by dimension

- **Use is treated as having no diagnostic value on its own.** The tiers contain no Use tier. The article says a program can report value in tiers "instead of a single usage figure", but does not say whether very low use is still a warning sign. E4 describes use and satisfaction as influencing net benefits, so use is not irrelevant to the later dimensions. The material does not discuss this.
- **Feedback from net benefits to use appears once.** E4 says net benefits feed back into later use and satisfaction. The leadership workaround (W5) is an instance: a manager's expectation about headcount shapes how much use is recorded. The article does not generalise from this. Its own measures can be affected in the same way. For example, "Workarounds found and removed can be reported from the first weeks" is itself a count of activity that a team under pressure could shape. This is my inference from W5, not a claim in the material.
- **Information quality is absent as a cause.** Workarounds attributed to data or context (C8, W3) are treated as design faults of the interface or workflow, so a data fault and a design fault are not separated.
- **User satisfaction is treated as a symptom of design, not as a measure.** C19 rejects explanations that blame people, which is a position, not a tested claim. The material has no data on whether resistance, engagement or compliance ever explain failed programs.
- **Cost and feasibility of the proposed measures.** Observing work and interviewing staff (T1) takes effort that adoption counts do not. The material does not address who bears it or how it scales.
- **Single cause.** The article attributes workarounds to design failure (C18, "Each workaround shows which part of the design failed"). Some of its own examples (W4, training time; W5, leadership incentives) are caused by workload and incentives, not interface or tool design. Its fixes for these (F4, F5) are accordingly not design fixes.

## Fit of the technique on this material

I could assign every item, but the assignment is not fully reliable. Fourteen of 38 items (37%) fit two categories. To test sensitivity, I counted the items whose Use placement depends on a close call (second category System quality, Net benefits or User satisfaction): C5, C6, C7, C11, C15, C20 and W5. If all seven moved to their second category, Use would fall from 16 to 9, System quality would rise to 12, Net benefits to 11 and User satisfaction to 2. Use would remain the largest or near-largest category, and Information quality would still have no primary entries. The finding about missing categories is therefore not sensitive to the borderline calls. Only one rater (me) assigned items, so agreement between raters is not tested.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumed that the six dimensions in E4 are a suitable lens for an article about measuring AI transformation. E4 was read through a search summary of DeLone and McLean (2003), so definitions of each dimension are my one-line versions based on that summary and on common use of the terms, not the authors' full wording.
- The model was built for information systems success, not for AI programs or for judging an essay. Applying it to an article's claims treats the article's statements as indicators, which is a stretch of its intended use.
- The item list is my selection of 38 items from the article. Another reader could split or merge items, which would change the counts. The counts describe how the article's statements are spread, not how important each is.
- Counts weigh a one-line claim and a list of five workaround levels equally per item. The article spends more words on System quality and workarounds than the counts show.
- The technique shows which kind of evidence is absent. It cannot test whether a claim is true. Whether organisations measure by adoption, and whether workarounds are as common as claimed, cannot be answered from the material.
- Only one rater assigned categories, and no second rating was done.

## Alternative explanations

- **The imbalance may reflect the article's purpose, not a weakness.** An argument that adoption measures are inadequate will naturally hold many Use items, and an opinion piece of this kind may not be expected to include Net benefits data. On this reading, the empty Net benefits evidence is a feature of the genre, and the gap matters only if the article is read as a research finding.
- **The absence of Information quality and User satisfaction items may be a matter of scope.** The article is about workarounds at the point of use, so data quality and attitudes may sit outside its question. The lens flags them as missing, but the article never claimed to cover the full model.
- **The workaround examples may come from the unnamed research mentioned in C17.** If so, the 38 items understate the evidence behind W1 to W5. The material cannot confirm or rule this out.
- **The clustering in System quality may reflect the author's own framework (WX) rather than the data.** The article introduces workflow experience as its own term, so the fixes may be sorted toward design because the framework is built around design.
