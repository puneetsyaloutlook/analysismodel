# Toulmin analysis of "Adopt an AI, today"

The article has no numbered items, so arguments are labelled A1 to A5 here and located by the section headings in the material. Each diagram gives the claim, ground, warrant, backing, rebuttal and qualifier, with the text that fills each part. Where the article does not state a part, the diagram says "not stated" and does not fill it with an invented version. Where a warrant is implied but not written, it is marked as inferred from the surrounding text.

## A1. Adoption measures cannot see workarounds (the central diagnosis)

Section: "People use a tool as designed or work around it", plus the five levels of workaround.

```
GROUND   "A workaround does not show up as non-use. The person still has the licence,
         still opens the tool and still counts as an active user, so on paper the
         tool has found a home."
         + five illustrated cases (interface, task, workflow, team, leadership)
            |
            v
CLAIM    "The work is done another way, and the usage figures cannot show the difference."
            ^
            |
WARRANT  (not stated; inferred) Adoption metrics count logins, features used and
         tokens, and none of these records how the work was actually completed.
         The first section does state what is counted: "how many people use an app
         or agent, how often they use its AI features, and how many tokens they consume."
            ^
            |
BACKING  Partial. "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic
         automation." No study, sample, date or count is given.

REBUTTAL Not stated. The article does not consider organisations whose adoption
         measures already include task completion, quality or acceptance rates.

QUALIFIER None. "Cannot show" and "has no way to record" are absolute. The article
         does not say how often workarounds occur.
```

Assessment. The claim follows from the ground if usage counts are what an adoption measure is. The article defines adoption that way in its second paragraph, so the argument is internally valid. The weak parts are the backing and the missing rebuttal. The five levels are described as "actual observations", but nothing in the material shows how many were seen, where, or how common each is. The article shows that workarounds can hide in usage data. It does not show that they do so often enough to explain failed programs.

## A2. Adoption cannot see the largest value, the removal of work

Section: "The largest gap is value that adoption cannot see at all."

```
GROUND   Two examples: "If a customer's documents update automatically when their
         circumstances change, nobody has to request them, process the request or
         answer the follow-up call." and "If a product is built so that a common
         error cannot happen, the team that checked for that error has nothing left
         to check."
            |
            v
CLAIM    "An adoption measure has no way to record this value."
         (stated as the conclusion of the section, and preceded by "The largest gap
         is value that adoption cannot see at all.")
            ^
            |
WARRANT  Stated in part: "nothing in it is being used more than it was before, in
         fact there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with."
         Unstated step: a measure that tracks use or a before and after comparison
         cannot record work that no longer exists.
            ^
            |
BACKING  Not stated. The two examples are hypothetical ("If ..."). No case, figure
         or source is cited.

REBUTTAL Not stated. The article does not consider that avoided work can be counted
         as demand removed (for example, fewer requests or follow-up calls), which
         a program could track without an earlier version of the work.

QUALIFIER "some of the most valuable changes". The claim about size ("the largest
         gap") is stronger than this qualifier, and no comparison of gap sizes is made.
```

Assessment. The reasoning is sound for usage measures. The word "largest" is not supported, because nothing in the material ranks this gap against the others. The warrant also contains a small inconsistency. The article says there is "no earlier version of the work to compare it with", but its own medium-term tier asks for tracking "compared with how it was done before". The two statements are reconcilable (the article may mean the AI component has no earlier version, not the process), but the text does not say so.

## A3. People do not "adopt AI"

Section: "People use tools and learn new jobs."

```
GROUND   Two cases. "In the first case, a person uses a tool that has some AI built
         into it." and "In the second case, a person learns a new job because some
         of the work is now done by agents."
         Plus two analogies: "Nobody describes booking a flight online as adopting
         ecommerce, or describes a new project management app as adopting the cloud."
         And the web search example.
            |
            v
CLAIM    "Neither case is adopting AI." and "A program built around adopting AI is
         organised around a technology that is becoming a component of everything."
            ^
            |
WARRANT  Not stated; inferred. If a technology is embedded in tools or absorbed into
         a job, then people are not choosing it, so "adoption" is the wrong unit of
         measurement.
            ^
            |
BACKING  The analogies (ecommerce, cloud, web search). No data. The search history
         is a single case.

REBUTTAL Not stated. The two cases are presented as exhaustive ("falls into two
         cases") without argument. Standalone AI tools that people choose, such as
         a chat assistant, fit neither case and are not discussed.

QUALIFIER "Taken literally, they do not." The literal reading is flagged, which
         leaves room for a looser use of the word.
```

Assessment. This is the least supported argument. The warrant is a definitional move, and the analogies show that mature technologies stop being described as adopted. They do not show that AI has reached that stage. The article also uses the future tense for the key step ("becoming"), while the claim is stated in the present tense. The two-case division is asserted, and a third case (people choosing a standalone AI tool) is left out. This matters because the article's own first section describes people consuming tokens in "an app or agent", which suggests direct use of AI products exists.

## A4. Workarounds show design failure, and explanations that blame people are wrong

Section: "Each workaround shows where the design failed", and the fixes by level.

```
GROUND   "People build workarounds because they are responsible for getting the work
         done and the tool as designed did not let them do it."
            |
            v
CLAIM    "Treating it as resistance discards that information. Training people to
         use the tool as designed will not help when the design is the reason they
         work around it."
            ^
            |
WARRANT  Stated: "Each workaround shows which part of the design failed, for whom,
         and what people do instead."
         Unstated step: the cause of a workaround is always, or mainly, the design,
         and not the person.
            ^
            |
BACKING  None beyond the earlier cases. The second ground sentence is itself a
         general claim about motive, and is not supported by evidence.

REBUTTAL Not stated. Some of the article's own examples are not design failures:
         the team that opens the tool daily because "nobody has had time to do the
         training" (a workload cause), and the manager who limits recorded usage
         to protect headcount (an incentive cause). The article does assign fixes
         for these (time, and a clear statement of how figures will be used), so
         it treats them as organisational, but it still lists them under the
         heading that says each workaround "shows where the design failed".

QUALIFIER None. "Each" is universal.
```

Assessment. The heading and the universal "each" overreach compared with the article's own examples. The team-level and leadership-level cases arise from workload and incentives, which the article handles in its fix list but not in its heading. A more accurate reading is that workarounds show where something in the system failed, with design as one source among several. The section also claims that common explanations "attribute the failure to people" and that "None of them questions the measures the program used". Both are asserted for programs in general with no evidence, so this is a description of what the author believes happens, not a finding.

## A5. Programs should report value in tiers instead of a single usage figure

Section: "What an AI program should measure and do instead."

```
GROUND   "A program still needs early evidence, because the value of AI takes time
         to show up in the numbers." Plus the earlier ground that adoption is used
         because it "can be counted from the first week of a rollout."
            |
            v
CLAIM    "Instead of a single usage figure, it can report value in tiers, from what
         can be shown in the first weeks to the end state the program is working
         towards"
            ^
            |
WARRANT  Stated: "none of these tiers is new. Each comes from established practice
         in building valuable solutions". Unstated step: practices that work for
         other solution builds will work for AI programs.
            ^
            |
BACKING  "These principles were true before AI and are still true now." and "we've
         done this before with methods that still work". No example, evidence of
         effect, or source is given.

REBUTTAL Partly stated. The article concedes that "AI is changing work in ways that
         are hard to picture in advance", which weakens the warrant that old methods
         transfer. It does not resolve this.

QUALIFIER "can report", "even when we cannot yet picture how bringing the AI home
         will eventually turn out." The proposal is offered as an option, and the
         final sentence admits uncertainty about the outcome.
```

Assessment. The short-term tier is the strongest part, because it addresses the original reason adoption was chosen (early reporting) with a measure that can also be reported early. The article's claim that workarounds "can be reported from the first weeks" is plausible but untested in the material, and it needs the observation and interviews the article describes, which are slower and less standardised than a usage count. The proposal does not say who would collect these observations or how results would be compared between programs. The tier list therefore answers the diagnosis but is not shown to work.

## Cross-cutting findings

Unstated or weakly stated warrants, ranked by how much weight they carry:

1. In A4, that a workaround is caused by design and not by the person, the workload or the incentives. This carries the article's main practical recommendation and is contradicted in part by its own examples.
2. In A3, that AI has already become, or is close enough to becoming, an embedded component that "adoption" is the wrong unit. The article's evidence is by analogy and is in the future tense.
3. In A1, that adoption measures in practice are only usage counts. This is stated in the opening, so it is a definition and not a hidden step, but the article gives no evidence that organisations measure adoption this narrowly.

Parts the article leaves out, as found in the diagrams:

- Evidence for how common workarounds are. The only backing is a one-line reference to "actual observations from research", with no source named.
- Any named organisation, study or figure for the opening claims that boards point AI at cost reduction and customer service, or that vendors and consultancies follow adoption measures.
- Cases where adoption measures include quality or outcome data.
- Any test of whether the tiered measures change outcomes.

The article's own admission about itself is useful: it calls adoption "itself a workaround", which applies the same logic to the measure. The argument is consistent on that point.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique was applied to the text as supplied. The material notes that the page could not be fetched and that the date and publisher are not given, so there is no way to check whether the text is complete or edited.
- Five arguments were identified by the author's section structure. Another reader might split or merge them, for example treating A3 as part of A1.
- Warrants marked "inferred" are my reconstruction from surrounding sentences. They could be wrong where the author held a different step in mind, which is the failure the technique record warns about. Where the article states a warrant in its own words, I quoted it.
- Toulmin analysis tests the structure of each argument. It does not check whether the facts in the grounds are true, and no external source was used, so the claims about boards, vendors, consultancies and the "actual observations" could not be verified.
- No counting or text cleaning was needed for this technique, so no code was run.

## Alternative explanations

- The missing backing in A1 may reflect the genre. This is a short opinion article for a general professional audience, and the author may hold research evidence that was left out for length. On this reading the article is not unsupported, only undocumented, and the diagnosis may be accurate.
- The universal wording in A4 ("each", "none") may be rhetorical emphasis, not a literal claim. Read that way, the argument is that design is the first place to look, and the team and leadership examples fit as secondary cases the article does address in its fix list.
- The weak case for A3 may be intended as a reframing and not as a proof. If the aim is to move readers from counting use to examining how work is done, the argument only needs the analogies to be suggestive, and its value would then lie in A1 and A5, which stand without A3.
