# Disconfirmation search on the favoured reading of the adoption article

## Claim under test

The seven earlier results (FQ-03, TC1-19, TC1-21, CM1-01, CM1-14, DM-07, TC1-13) jointly favour this reading, which I restate as three parts so each can be tested.

- Part A (narrow diagnosis). A usage count (users, frequency, tokens) cannot tell designed use from a workaround, and cannot record value that comes from removing work. This follows from what a usage count records.
- Part B (empirical premises). The article asserts, and does not show, that organisations mostly report adoption as progress, that workarounds are common, and that the five-level examples are real observations.
- Part C (point of view). The article overreaches when it says every workaround shows a design failure, when it treats adoption as a literal error, and when it offers its tiers as a remedy without evidence that they work.

The brief needs a description only, and the stakes are exploratory, so the aim is to record what would contradict the reading and whether it turned up, not to prove the reading.

## What would refute the claim (written before searching)

The following list was fixed before I searched the article or the evidence files.

| ID | Evidence that would refute the claim | Part tested |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | The article names a study, sample, count or organisation for its workaround examples or for its statements about boards, vendors and consultancies | B |
| R2 | The article, E1 or E2 shows high usage going together with good outcomes, or shows organisations already measuring outcomes | A and B |

| R8 | The article supports its claim that AI will become a component of everything, or that programs blame people when design is the cause | C |

## Search and results by item

The search was run over the article text (read in full), the seven findings (read in full) and E1 to E3. External sources were not used, because the technique needs none and the rules limit the work to the material. Text searches were literal matches, so a zero count prompted a second read and not a conclusion.

### R1: a source for the workaround examples or the premises

- Search used: literal searches of the article for "survey", "study", "%", "research", "evidence" and "already", then a full read of the opening paragraph and the workaround section.
- Result: not found. "survey", "study", "%" and "already" each return zero lines. "research" returns one line: "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation." It names no study, setting, sample or date. "evidence" appears twice, once about what people explain failure by and once in "A program still needs early evidence", and neither supplies a source. Every earlier finding that checked this (TC1-19, TC1-21, CM1-14, DM-07) reached the same result independently.
- Bearing: this item is the main support for Part B and was not refuted.

### R2: high usage with good outcomes, or outcomes already measured

- Search used: searches of the article for "outcome", "correlat" and "already", then a read of E1 and E2 for any result linking usage to results.
- Result: not found in the article. The word "outcome" appears three times, only in the proposed fixes. No passage describes a programme that measures outcomes today, or a case of usage and results moving together. E1 does not help in either direction: it records that developers believed AI had cut their time by 20% while tasks took 19% longer, so belief and result diverged in one small trial, which is one case and is not about usage counts. E2 reports that 75% of knowledge workers use AI and that 79% of leaders agree adoption is critical, which shows a priority on adoption and says nothing about how it is measured or whether it tracks results.
- Bearing: nothing refutes the claim. The absence of a counterexample is also a gap in the article (it never tests its own claim against cases where usage did match value), as CM1-14 (A3) and FQ-03 noted. I do not read this as confirmation of the article, only as a failure to find a refuter in the material.

### R3: usage data would reveal the workarounds

- Search used: a re-read of the five workaround levels against the sentence "A workaround does not show up as non-use", then a check of E2.
- Result: found in part. The article's own interface example says "the approved tool is slow, so people use a personal one instead". That behaviour would show as low use of the approved tool, which is what a usage count does detect. E2 reports that 78% of AI users bring their own AI tools to work, and that fits this example. The statement "A workaround does not show up as non-use" is therefore true for some workarounds (parallel running, token use then finishing by hand, daily token opening) and false for others (substitution by a personal tool, abandonment). FQ-03 noted this for personal tools and abandonment, and TC1-21 (entry 7) noted that the two-way split leaves out non-use. The article's own claim is broader than its examples support.
- Bearing: this partly refutes Part A as I stated it, because the narrow diagnosis holds only for workarounds that keep the tool open. It does not refute the core logic, because the parallel-running and ten-attempt cases still follow from how counts work. The favoured reading should say "some workarounds", not "workarounds".

### R4: removed-work value can be measured by comparison

- Search used: a read of the article's "largest gap" paragraph and the medium-term tier, then a check of FQ-03, TC1-19 and TC1-21 for the same point.
- Result: found in part. The article says removed work has "no earlier version of the work to compare it with", yet its medium-term tier says to track "time, quality, errors, and the experience of staff and customers for each piece of work the program changes, compared with how it was done before". FQ-03 observed that earlier request volumes and the checking team's workload could serve as a baseline. The article also says that for sped-up steps "the same work now takes less time and can be compared with last year's figures". So the article's remedy implies that some comparison is possible, which weakens the claim that this value is invisible, though only to usage counts, which is what the article actually says ("An adoption measure has no way to record this value"). The claim about usage counts is untouched.
- Bearing: the claim that this value cannot be recorded by adoption measures survives. The stronger reading that such value is unmeasurable by any method is contradicted by the article's own remedy. Three findings (FQ-03, TC1-19, TC1-21) already flag the tension.

### R5: support for the design-failure claim or the two-case split

- Search used: a read of the team and leadership examples and their stated fixes, and of the passage "falls into two cases".
- Result: not found. The team example (nobody had time for training) and the leadership example (a manager manages recorded usage) are failures of workload and incentives. Their stated fixes are "time to learn the new way of working" and "a clear statement of how adoption figures will and will not be used", which are not changes to the tool. The two-case split is asserted as complete with no argument. TC1-19 and TC1-21 reached the same result.
- Bearing: Part C was not refuted. A defence exists (design in a wide sense that includes work organisation), but it is the article's WX idea and the article does not state it in this text.

### R6: a finding that contradicts the joint reading, or misquotes the article

- Search used: a comparison of the seven findings' conclusions against each other, and a check of quoted passages against the article for the ones I relied on.
- Result: not found as a contradiction, with two points of difference in weight.
  - TC1-13 favours H2 (plausible but over-asserted) over H3 and H4 by a narrow margin that rests on one vendor item (E2b), and TC1-13 says so. The ordering of H2, H3 and H4 is therefore not firm. TC1-13's separation of H1 (the article is fully supported) from the others is firmer.
  - TC1-21 entry 11 raises a possible inconsistency between "few organisations can trace either back to AI specifically" and "the gain can be measured" for sped-up steps. It treats the two as possibly reconcilable (measuring a gain differs from attributing it to AI), and I agree, so this does not contradict the joint reading.
  - The quotations I checked match the article. I did not re-check every quotation in every finding.
- Bearing: the findings agree on the shape of the conclusion. They are not independent in the strong sense, because they all read one article and four of them (TC1-19, TC1-21, CM1-14, DM-07) share the same missing-source observation, which counts as one observation made several times and not as several confirmations.

### R7: the evidence files contradict the mechanism or show the proposals work

- Search used: a read of E1, E2 and E3 for any result bearing on workarounds, concealment, attribution, or the effect of outcome measures.
- Result: found in part, in three places, none of which refutes the diagnosis.
  - E1 shows that after the tasks the developers still believed AI had cut time by 20%, when it had increased time by 19%. This weakens the article's short-term tier, which relies partly on asking people what they do ("ask people what they do when a tool does not give them what they need"), because self-report was wrong in this trial. The tier also says to observe the work, and E1 does not test observation. E1 covers 16 developers in one task type with early-2025 tools.
  - E2's 53% worry about revealing AI use fits concealment, but concealment of use would make usage counts understate use, not overstate effect. It also bears on the proposed tier: if people hide use for fear of being replaceable, observation and interviews face the same distortion the article attributes to usage figures. E2 is a vendor survey whose method I did not read, and it records worry, not behaviour.
  - E3 says that earlier measures of IT productivity were "almost impossible to assess" and the evidence was inconclusive, and that new data later gave positive results. That fits the idea that measures can be part of the problem. It gives no evidence on workarounds, and it shows that established methods did not settle the matter for years, which weakens the article's closing line that "we've done this before with methods that still work" (CM1-01, element 12).
- Bearing: no item contradicts Part A. E1 and E2 give weak reasons to doubt the remedy (Part C), which the earlier findings already identified. None shows that the proposed tiers would work or fail.

### R8: support for the prediction about AI as a component, or for the people-blaming claim

- Search used: a read of the web search passage and of "When an adoption does not work out".
- Result: not found. The prediction rests on one analogy (web search). The claim about common explanations ("staff are resisting, that leaders are not engaged enough, or that the organisation did not follow the program") has no source or case. The article does not name a programme that blamed people when design was at fault.
- Bearing: Part C stands.

## What the findings mean for the claim

The favoured reading survived the search with one correction and two caveats.

- Correction. Part A should be narrowed. Usage counts hide workarounds that keep the tool open (parallel running, repeated attempts followed by manual finishing, daily token opening). They do not hide a workaround that replaces the tool or an abandoned tool, which appear as low use. The article's sentence "A workaround does not show up as non-use" is wider than its own examples.
- Caveat on Part A and the remedy. The claim that value from removed work is invisible holds for usage counts. It does not hold for measurement in general, as the article's own medium-term tier shows.
- Caveat on independence. The agreement among the seven findings is weaker than seven separate confirmations, because the findings share one article and one evidence set, and the missing-source point recurs in four of them.

Parts B and C were not refuted. Nothing in the article or the three evidence files I read supplies a source for the premises, a counterexample, or support for the design-failure claim. That is a statement about what this search found in a small body of material. It does not show that such evidence does not exist elsewhere, and it does not show that the article is right.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The refuter list is mine and may be incomplete. I wrote it before searching, but it reflects what I judged an informed critic would raise. A different list, for example one that asks whether the article's examples match published studies of enterprise workarounds, would need external material, which the rules excluded.
- The search covers a small body of text: one article of roughly 2,000 words, seven findings and three evidence summaries. A finding of "not found" here is weak evidence about the world. This is the failure the technique warns about, and I have not treated it as confirmation.
- E1, E2 and E3 were read only as search summaries, and E2 is a vendor survey whose method was not read. E4 was not read, because the instruction named E1 to E3.
- Text searches were literal and would miss paraphrase. I read the relevant passages in full after each search, but a refuting phrase in other words could still have been missed.
- The article text was supplied because the page could not be fetched, and it gives no date or publisher. It may differ from the published version.
- I did not re-run the earlier techniques. Where I rely on a finding, I rely on its stated reading of the article and checked its quotations selectively.

## Alternative explanations

- The search may have found little because the article is a practitioner's position piece that does not set out to prove prevalence. On that reading, the missing sources reflect genre, and the right test is plausibility and usefulness, which the narrow diagnosis passes.
- The partial refutations (R3, R4) may reflect loose wording and not faulty reasoning. "A workaround does not show up as non-use" may be meant for the cases where the person keeps using the tool, and "no earlier version" may refer to a single removed task and not to a whole programme. If so, the correction to Part A is a matter of phrasing.
- The agreement among the earlier findings may be partly produced by the shared evidence set and shared framing. A reader starting from a different lens, for example organisational incentives, might weigh the team and leadership examples as the article's strongest material and the tool-design claim as the weakest, which would change the emphasis but not the facts found here.
- The lack of any counterexample (R2) could mean such cases are rare, or only that the article and the evidence summaries were not built to include them. The material cannot separate these.
