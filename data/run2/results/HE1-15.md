# Content analysis of eight voice AI interviews against H1 to H3

## Coding scheme and method

The unit of analysis is one quote (Q1 to Q10). A quote can receive more than one code. Codes were set in advance from the wording of the three hypotheses, plus two categories that emerged from the material (E1, E2). One coder (this analysis) applied the scheme, so there is no agreement check, and the counts below are not reliability-tested.

Counts are only over the ten supplied quotes from six participants (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P10). P8 and P9 have no quotes in the material, so they are uncoded and their views are unknown. A participant who is not quoted on a topic is not counted as holding a view on it.

Coding notes that affect the counts:

- Q5 is coded twice. "at first" the speaker would want to speak to someone (H3a), and later calls it "a really good process" (H1a).
- P1 appears in both H1a (Q1) and H1b (Q6), so participant counts for H1a and H1b overlap and must not be added.
- Q4 is coded unfavourable because the speaker objects to the AI voice and its American accent. It does not state dissatisfaction in those words.
- Q9 and Q10 are not coded on H1, because neither expresses an evaluation of the interaction.
- Q2 and Q3 are coded as favourable comparisons because they contrast the AI with a "robot voice" and with having "to repeat yourself". Q1 compares it with "chat bots", which are text tools and not voice automation.

## What the frequencies show for each hypothesis

**H1: more satisfying than traditional voice automation.** Mixed within this sample. Three participants (P1, P2, P3) compare the AI favourably with earlier systems, and P4 comes round to it. Three participants (P1, P5, P10) express dissatisfaction. No quote uses the word "satisfying", and none compares the AI with a traditional voice automation (IVR) system by name. The closest comparisons are Q2 (robot voice) and Q3 (having to repeat yourself). Q1's comparison is with chatbots. The material therefore shows a link between favourable views and comparison with earlier tools, but it does not show that the AI is more satisfying than traditional voice automation. Two of the unfavourable views (Q7, Q4) concern voice character and a stressful context, which the favourable quotes do not address.

**H2: customers do not hang up immediately on learning they are speaking with an AI.** Not tested directly. No quote reports a hang up, and none says a participant hung up on learning it was an AI. Two quotes state a hang up intention. Q8 ("I would've definitely have hung up at the intro") gives length of the introduction as the reason, not the AI disclosure. Q10 makes hanging up conditional on not getting a human. The material does not say whether the introduction is where the AI is disclosed. Evidence against H2 is therefore weak and indirect: two of six quoted participants say they would have hung up, under conditions that do not match the hypothesis wording. No participant says the opposite.

**H3: customers do not immediately ask for a human agent.** Counter-evidence exists. P4 says (Q5) that at first they would want to speak to someone, and Q9 says "If I was gonna do it, that's probably where I would do it", said about asking for a human at the introduction. P5 says in Q10 "I would've asked for a human". That is two of six quoted participants, with three quotes. P4's later reassurance (Q5) shows the wish can fade as the call proceeds. All of these are stated intentions after listening to prerecorded scenarios, not observed behaviour, so none shows an actual request. No quote shows a participant choosing not to ask.

## Pattern across codes

H3a and H2a overlap on P5 (Q10), and E2 overlaps with both through P4 and P10. Taken together, the codes that bear on hanging up and requesting a human cluster at the introduction (Q8, Q9) and at P5's accident scenario (Q7, Q10). The favourable codes (H1a, H1c) come from P1, P2, P3 and P4, none of whom state an intention to hang up or ask for a human, other than P4's first reaction in Q5. This is an association within six participants only.

## Agreement between coders

Not applicable. Only one coder was used, so no agreement statistic is reported, and the borderline codings above (Q4, Q5, Q6, Q8) are unchecked judgements.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumes a coding scheme fixed before reading. The scheme here was derived from the hypotheses and so tends to find material that matches them. Two emergent codes (E1, E2) were added after reading.
- Counting assumes each quote is a comparable unit. The quotes are selected extracts ("Only some participants are quoted") in no particular order, so frequencies measure the selected quotes, not how often each view arose across the interviews.
- P8 and P9 have no quotes, so the counts cover six of the eight participants. Absence of a quote is not absence of a view.
- Coding removes context. Q7 is hypothetical (a car accident), Q8 and Q10 are conditional, and Q5 changes over time. A single category label hides these differences, so the table should be read alongside the quote text.
- The material cannot answer whether any participant hung up or asked for a human in practice, whether the AI disclosure falls in the introduction, or how participants rated traditional voice automation.
- No coder agreement check was possible, and no significance can be claimed from ten quotes.

## Alternative explanations

- The favourable and unfavourable views may reflect different scenarios heard, not different attitudes to voice AI. Each participant heard four scenarios, but the material does not say which scenario each quote refers to. Q7 is about a motor claim and could differ from views on sales or cancellation.
- The hang up and human-request codes may reflect dislike of a long introduction (Q8, Q9) more than dislike of AI. If so, H2 and H3 would be affected by the design of the introduction, not by the AI disclosure.
- The favourable quotes (Q1 to Q3) may be favourable because the participants' expectations were low from earlier chatbot experience, so the AI improves on a low baseline without being more satisfying than voice automation.
- Participants may state hypothetical intentions more strongly than they would act. The stated hang up and human-request intentions could therefore overstate real behaviour, which would weaken the counter-evidence to H2 and H3.
