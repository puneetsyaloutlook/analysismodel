# Absence analysis of "Adopt an AI, today: a workaround for measuring AI transformation"

## Scope and method

The claim tested is the article's diagnosis: organisations measure AI transformation by adoption, and adoption measures miss workarounds and value. The reference structure is the DeLone and McLean model (E4), which has six dimensions (information quality, system quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, net benefits) and keeps use separate from net benefits. A discussion of measuring AI success that was complete against this model would be expected to touch each dimension. I also list items that any argument resting on an empirical diagnosis would be expected to contain, whatever the model says.

I counted terms in the article text (about 2,050 words) with a text search to check which expected items appear at all. Relevant counts: "adopt" 28, "workaround" 20, "measure" 8, "value" 6, "interface" 4, "training" 3, "outcome" 3, "trust" 2, "evidence" 2, "research" 1, "quality" 1, "data" 1, "cost" 1; "satisf", "survey", "study", "baseline", "benefit", "govern", "safety", "security", "privacy" and "feedback" each 0. A zero count is only a prompt to read the text again, because the article can cover an idea in other words.

## What the model says is present

Most DeLone and McLean dimensions are covered, in the article's own terms:

| Dimension | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Use | The whole diagnosis: adoption "counts use". |
| Net benefits | "whether the work is done better, faster or more reliably, which is what the original investment was for", and the medium term tier of time, quality, errors and experience. |
| System quality | The interface and speed workarounds ("acceptable speed"). |
| Information quality | One phrase: "how good its data is". |
| Service quality | Only indirectly, as training time and a named accountable person for team workarounds. |
| User satisfaction | Only indirectly, as trust and "the experience of staff and customers". |

The article's separation of use from value matches the model's separation of use from net benefits. That supports the diagnosis that use is a weak stand-in for benefit, but E4 as summarised does not say adoption measures are the dominant practice, so it cannot confirm that part.

## What the absences say about the brief

On accuracy of the diagnosis, the missing items are sourcing items (A1, A2). The diagnosis that adoption counts use and not value is supported by the article's reasoning, and E4 supports separating use from net benefits. But the premise that organisations in practice measure this way, and the frequency of workarounds, rest on assertion.

On the validity of the point of view, the strongest absences are A2 and A5. The article asks the reader to accept workaround observations it does not describe, and it proposes outcome measures without confronting the attribution problem it raises in its first paragraph.

What the article leaves out that the model would expect is limited. Information quality, system quality, service quality and net benefits are reachable in the text, and satisfaction is implied. The absence of risk, governance and security (A9) and of explicit satisfaction (A10) is weak evidence of an omission, because the article does not claim to give a complete account of AI success.

## Conclusion

The absences count as limited evidence. They are strong only where the article makes an empirical claim and gives no basis for it (A1, A2) or where it raises a problem and does not apply it to its own proposal (A5). None of the absences indicates concealment. Several are consistent with a short opinion piece that is not meant to be a complete account. The honest reading is that the reasoning is coherent but the evidence for its premises is thin and not shown.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumes each item is expected for a stated reason. For A1, A2 and A5 the reason comes from the article's own claims. For A9 to A11 the reason comes only from E4 and from my judgement of what an AI measurement piece normally covers, so those are weaker.
- E4 was read as a summary only (abstract and descriptions in other papers), so I could not check how the model defines each dimension in detail.
- The term counts are literal text matches and do not capture paraphrase. I read the article in full to confirm each status.
- The technique cannot say whether the research claimed in A2 exists, only that the article does not show it.
- The fit test says absence is weak evidence when the author did not intend a complete account. This is a short opinion style piece of unknown date and publisher, so the weight of A3, A4 and A7 to A11 in particular is low.
- No external sources on how common adoption based measurement is were used, as the rules limit this to the material.

## Alternative explanations

- The article may be written as a position piece and not as a study. Read that way, the missing sources (A1, A2) are a genre choice, and its claims are offered as a design argument to be tested by the reader's own experience.
- The omission of risk, governance and satisfaction (A9, A10) may reflect a deliberate focus on design and workflow, which the author labels "WX", and not a gap in thinking.
- The missing counterexample (A3) and missing attribution problem (A5) could be a result of space (about 2,050 words) and not of one sided reasoning.
- The article's final paragraph says the tiers come from established practice, so the lack of a trial for them (A4) could be seen as intentional: the author is relying on prior evidence for those practices, which the article does not cite but which exists outside the material.
