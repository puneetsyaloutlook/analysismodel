# Fallacy analysis of "Adopt an AI, today: a workaround for measuring AI transformation"

The material carries no item IDs, so passages are referred to by the article's own section headings (opening, "People use tools and learn new jobs", "People use a tool as designed or work around it", "Workarounds happen at every level, from the interface to team leadership", "Each workaround shows where the design failed", "What an AI program should measure and do instead"). Quotations are copied exactly from the article.

Every entry below is a prompt for missing support. None is proof that the claim is false.

## Argument in explicit form

The article does not set out premises and conclusion separately, so the reconstruction below is mine and is the basis for the entries that follow.

- P1. Value from AI is slow to appear in financial results, so programs report adoption, which can be counted early.
- P2. Adoption counts use. Use and effective work can differ, and a workaround still registers as use.
- P3. Some of the most valuable changes remove work, so there is nothing to count as use.
- P4. Adoption assumes people "adopt AI", but people only use tools with AI inside them or learn new jobs.
- P5. People work around tools because the design failed, and programs that cannot see workarounds blame people.
- C1 (diagnosis). Adoption measures miss workarounds and value.
- C2 (point of view). Programs should measure workarounds, then work quality, then new jobs, then progress to an end state.

## Suspected defects

### 1. Unsupported general claim about what organisations do (opening)

- Passage: "Boards mostly point AI at cost reduction and customer service, and executives report productivity gains." The opening also says adoption "seems to have gone mainstream".
- Pattern or rule: hasty generalisation, and breach of the pragma-dialectical burden of proof rule (a standpoint that is put forward must be defended when challenged).
- Missing support: any survey, case or named source showing what boards and executives mostly do, or how widely adoption is used as the progress measure. P1 is the starting point of the diagnosis, so C1 depends on it.

### 2. Causal order asserted without evidence (opening)

- Passage: "Following their lead, vendors set their pricing based on seats and usage, and consultancies build their frameworks around it."
- Pattern or rule: possible questionable cause (the order of influence is assumed).
- Missing support: evidence that vendor pricing and consultancy frameworks followed programs rather than the reverse, or arose independently. The article does not need this claim for C1, so its effect is on credibility, not on the logic.

### 3. Evidence announced but not provided (workarounds at every level)

- Passage: "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation."
- Pattern or rule: appeal to unnamed authority, and breach of the burden of proof rule.
- Missing support: the research, its sample, its setting and how often each workaround was seen. The five-level typology and the task example ("Staff who see the review as unnecessary change details in the underlying records so that the case no longer triggers it...") rest on this sentence alone. The material cannot show whether the observations are real or how common they are.

### 4. Possibility presented as the usual case (adoption problems list)

- Passage: "Use and effective work can move in opposite directions." The support is one illustration of a person needing ten attempts.
- Pattern or rule: hasty generalisation from a constructed instance.
- Missing support: evidence of how often usage and effective work diverge. The claim that a divergence can occur is supported. The diagnosis needs it to occur often enough to make adoption measures misleading, and the article does not say how often.

### 5. Straw man on the meaning of "adopt" (People use tools and learn new jobs)

- Passage: "AI adoption assumes that people actually "adopt AI". Taken literally, they do not." The article then argues "Nobody describes booking a flight online as adopting ecommerce, or describes a new project management app as adopting the cloud."
- Pattern or rule: straw man (a literal reading is attacked that adoption programs may not hold), with a universal claim ("Nobody") and an analogy.
- Missing support: a showing that adoption measures depend on the literal reading, and evidence that the ecommerce and cloud comparisons are close enough to AI. The article itself describes adoption as "borrowed from earlier software rollouts, where success meant getting people to use the new system", which suggests the term is used in an ordinary organisational sense, not a literal one. The article's own point stands without this step, because the two-case typology does not need the word to be literally wrong.

### 6. Analogy and extrapolation from web search (People use tools and learn new jobs)

- Passage: "Web search followed this path." and "A program built around adopting AI is organised around a technology that is becoming a component of everything."
- Pattern or rule: false analogy and prediction stated as present fact.
- Missing support: reasons to expect AI to follow search, and evidence that it already has. The second case in the typology (a person who "is learning a job, with new tasks, judgements and accountabilities") is not a component that is becoming ordinary, so the claim covers only the first case.

### 7. Two-option split that leaves out other cases (As designed or workaround)

- Passage: "Faced with any tool at work, a person does one of two things. They use it as designed, or they find a workaround that gets the job done." The article adds "A workaround is its opposite."
- Pattern or rule: false dilemma, and a definition that is stipulated and then relied on (see entry 8).
- Missing support: treatment of mixed use, partial use, genuine non-use, and use that is neither as designed nor a workaround that gets the job done. Genuine non-use matters because the article's own claim is that a workaround "does not show up as non-use", which implies non-use is something usage figures do detect.

### 8. Circular definition of adoption (As designed or workaround)

- Passage: "If adoption means anything at work, it means the tool works well enough, as designed, for people to get the job done with it."
- Pattern or rule: begging the question (the conclusion is built into the definition).
- Missing support: a reason to define adoption this way rather than as use. If adoption is defined to exclude workarounds, then it follows that counting use cannot measure it, so C1 on this point is true by definition. The factual part, that a person with a licence who opens the tool still counts as an active user, is separately sound (see the passages not flagged).

### 9. Sweeping claim that every workaround shows design failure (Each workaround shows where the design failed)

- Passage: "Each workaround shows which part of the design failed, for whom, and what people do instead."
- Pattern or rule: hasty generalisation, and an inconsistency with the article's own cases.
- Missing support: the article's team example (no time for training, so the team opens the tool each day to record usage) and its leadership example (a manager keeps recorded usage low enough to protect the team) are caused by workload and incentives. The remedies given for them are training time and a statement about how figures will be used, not a change to the tool. The claim fits the interface, task and workflow cases better than the team and leadership cases.

### 10. Blanket claim about how failures are explained (Each workaround shows where the design failed)

- Passage: "Each explanation attributes the failure to people. None of them questions the measures the program used or the design of the tools it introduced." The article continues "so when the work does not improve it has no evidence about the design and looks for the cause in people instead."
- Pattern or rule: straw man and false dichotomy between people and design, plus an unsupported causal claim about how programs behave.
- Missing support: evidence of how programs actually explain failure. "The organisation did not follow the program" is arguably a claim about the program, not about people. The leadership example in the article itself locates a cause in a person's choice, so the article does not treat person-level causes as always wrong.

### 11. Tension between two statements about measuring value (opening and The largest gap)

- Passage: the opening says "few organisations can trace either back to AI specifically", while a later section says "When AI speeds up the steps of an existing process, the gain can be measured".
- Pattern or rule: inconsistency between premises.
- Missing support: an account of when attribution to AI is possible. The two statements may be reconcilable (measuring a gain differs from attributing it to AI), but the article does not say so.

### 12. Superlative and unclear claim about value that removes work (The largest gap)

- Passage: "The largest gap is value that adoption cannot see at all." and "AI may be part of how either change works, but nothing in it is being used more than it was before, in fact there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with."
- Pattern or rule: unsupported comparison, plus a premise that is hard to follow.
- Missing support: a comparison showing this gap is larger than the others, and evidence beyond two hypothetical cases. If AI is part of how the change works, it is in use, so the statement that nothing is "being used more than it was before" needs explaining. The conclusion that removed work cannot be recorded as use is plausible for the examples given, but the claim of size is not supported.

### 13. The proposed replacement may share the flaw it criticises (What an AI program should measure)

- Passage: "Workarounds found and removed can be reported from the first weeks of a rollout, which is when adoption figures are reported now." The method is "Observe the work as it is done, ask people what they do when a tool does not give them what they need".
- Pattern or rule: double standard (a proxy is faulted for being easy to report, then a new reportable proxy is offered), and a possible gap in the premises about concealment.
- Missing support: why a count of workarounds found is a better indicator than a count of use, and how observation and questioning would reach workarounds that the article says people hide (the team and leadership examples). Neither the article nor the material gives evidence that the tiers have worked in practice.

### 14. Appeal to established practice (closing paragraph)

- Passage: "One look at that list, and it's obvious: none of these tiers is new." and "we've done this before with methods that still work".
- Pattern or rule: appeal to tradition or common practice, and an appeal to obviousness in place of support.
- Missing support: evidence that the older methods worked for the kinds of change the article describes, given its own statement that "AI is changing work in ways that are hard to picture in advance." The closing also does not explain why programs use adoption if the better methods are well known. The article's answer (adoption is borrowed from earlier software rollouts) is given, but it is not tested.

### 15. Workers assumed to be right (Task workarounds and the fix list)

- Passage: "Task workarounds need checks that ask for real judgement, not approval." This follows the task example in which staff edit records so a review is not triggered.
- Pattern or rule: assumption that the person working around the tool has judged correctly (a form of begging the question).
- Missing support: a way to tell a workaround that exposes a poor review step from one that removes a needed control. The article's premise is that people "are responsible for getting the work done", which does not show they judge the review correctly.

## Passages checked and not flagged

- A person who keeps a licence, opens the tool and still works the old way will count as an active user ("A workaround does not show up as non-use"). The conclusion follows from how usage is counted, so no fallacy is suspected in the logic. The frequency of this behaviour is a separate matter (entries 3 and 4).
- The statement that usage figures "cannot show what makes an app or agent useful" follows from what usage figures contain.
- The claim that value from removed work has no earlier version to compare with is coherent for the two examples given, apart from the points in entry 12.
- The closing remark that adoption "is itself a workaround" is a deliberate extension of the article's own term and is not flagged as a defect in reasoning, although it stretches the earlier definition.

## What the entries show

The narrow diagnosis, that usage counts cannot distinguish designed use from workarounds or capture work that no longer exists, follows from how usage is counted. The weaker parts are the empirical premises (entries 1, 3, 4), the definition and typology steps (entries 5, 7, 8, 9), and the proposed solution (entries 13, 14). The argument leaves out evidence of prevalence, evidence that the proposed tiers work, and the cases (non-use, partial use, useful workarounds) that do not fit its two options.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The article is not written as premises and conclusion, so the technique's minimum requirement was met only through my reconstruction. A different reconstruction could change which entries apply.
- The technique checks the form of the argument against known defect patterns. It cannot test whether the article's factual claims are true, and the material holds nothing outside the article to test them against.
- The article is a position piece on a general-audience platform. Missing citations may be a feature of the genre and not a sign of weak reasoning, and the publisher and date are not given.
- Labels were applied only where a passage and a gap in support could both be pointed to. Some entries (2, 5, 6) are weaker than others and are closer to questions than to defects.
- No counting or text analysis was called for, so no code was run.

## Alternative explanations

- The many missing-support entries may reflect the genre, not weak reasoning. A practitioner essay that argues for a way of thinking can reasonably rely on plausible examples, and the core claim (a usage count cannot see a workaround) needs only possibility, not prevalence. On this reading the empirical gaps matter less than entries 1, 3 and 4 suggest.
- The straw man and false dilemma entries (5, 7, 10) may be deliberate simplification for a typology, where two cases are a teaching device and not a claim that nothing else exists. The article does not say this, so the reader cannot tell which it is.
- The tension in entries 9 and 11 may come from the article using "design" and "measure" in a wide sense that includes workload and incentives. If so, the claim "each workaround shows where the design failed" would be consistent, but only with a wider meaning of design than the article states.
