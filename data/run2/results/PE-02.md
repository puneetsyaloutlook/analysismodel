# Preprocessing sensitivity test on the eight interviews

## Question and what was counted

The brief asks whether the interviews support H1 (voice AI is more satisfying than traditional voice automation), H2 (customers do not hang up immediately on learning it is an AI) and H3 (customers do not immediately ask for a human). The technique converts the ten quotes (Q1 to Q10, six of the eight participants: P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P10; P8 and P9 have no quote in the material) into countable tokens and re-runs one result under six cleaning regimes. Nothing here exceeds patterns within this sample.

The main result tested is a fixed cue lexicon, written before counting, applied to each quote. The lexicon is matched on word starts so that stemming does not break it.

- H1 positive cues: seamless, excellent, advanced, dynamic, real, actual, easy, exactly, good.
- H1 negative cues: annoy, frustrat, cold, clinical, robot, long.
- H2 cue: hung, hang.
- H3 cues: human, someone.

The result is the number of quotes (out of 10) containing each cue group, plus the top five terms by frequency. Code was Python 3 with only the standard library. No tokeniser or stopword package was installed, so a regular expression tokeniser and a hand-written English stopword list were used. The suffix stemmer is crude (it turns "seamless" into "seamles" and "process" into "proces"), which is why cues are matched by prefix.

## Log of cleaning rules

Every regime starts with these rules: curly apostrophes made straight; quote text only (the ID and participant code are excluded); tokens are runs of letters and apostrophes; single-character tokens dropped; Q1 to Q10 treated as ten separate documents.

| Rule | R1 | R2 | R3 | R4 | R5 | R6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lowercase | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Stopword removal (about 120 function words, includes not, no, nor, didn't) | no | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Negations (not, no, nor, never, didn't) kept despite stopword removal | not applicable | no | no | no | yes | yes |
| Suffix stemming (ingly, edly, ing, ed, ly, es, s when 4 or more letters remain) | no | no | yes | yes | no | yes |
| Transcript fillers and hedges removed (um, uh, like, yeah, okay, kind, stuff, really, you, know, definitely, probably, sometimes, actually, seems, seemed, think, just) | no | no | no | yes | no | yes |

Tokens retained: R1 355, R2 183, R3 183, R4 130, R5 186, R6 133.

## What the cue quotes say when read

Counting is only a locator. Reading the matched quotes changes how they should be used.

- H1: Q1 contrasts the AI with older chatbots ("this one seems to be pretty dynamic"), Q2 says it "seemed like talking to a real person" and Q3 says "that was really excellent". These three (P1, P2, P3) favour the AI. Q5 (P4) starts reluctant and ends positive ("a really good process"). The negative cue in Q2 is the word "robot" used in a comparison, so the lexicon wrongly counts Q2 as negative. Q6 (P1), Q7 (P5) and Q8 (P10) are unfavourable. Q4 (P10) matched no cue but says "this sounds very much like an AI voice". No quote compares satisfaction with traditional voice automation directly; Q1 compares with website chatbots, which is a different thing. H1 is only partly addressed.
- H2: Q8 and Q10 are the only hang cues, from P10 and P5. Both are hypothetical ("I would've definitely have hung up at the intro."; "I probably would've hung up and then tried to do it manually online."). No quote reports an actual hang-up. The cue count of 2 shows two of six quoted participants state they would hang up, so H2 is contradicted for those two and untested for P1, P2, P3 and P4.
- H3: Q5, Q9 and Q10 are the human-request cues that matter. P4 says "I'd be probably wanting to speak to someone" and, about the introduction, "If I was gonna do it, that's probably where I would do it". P5 says "I would've asked for a human." Q6 (P1) matched "human" but reports that a human option was offered, not a request. So two of six quoted participants (P4, P5) say they would ask for a human at or near the start, and H3 is contradicted for them.

## Whether themes or rankings changed across regimes

The cue counts did not change: every cell is identical across R1 to R6, so the link between quotes and each hypothesis is stable to these cleaning choices. The word-frequency ranking did change. The top five in R1 are function words, in R2 to R3 are dominated by the fillers "um" and "like", and only from R4 (fillers removed) does a substantive set (ai, voice, talk) appear. Rankings of raw frequent words are therefore unstable and should not be used for themes here; the fixed cue lexicon is stable.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The lexicon was chosen by me from reading the ten quotes, so it fits this sample by construction. Stable counts across regimes show insensitivity to cleaning, not that the lexicon is valid.
- Bag-of-words ignores tense, conditionals and contrast. It cannot separate a hypothetical hang-up from a real one, a request from a noted option (Q6), or "robot" in praise from "robot" in complaint (Q2).
- Ten short quotes is too little text for an unsupervised model, so none was run. Whether neural embedding methods are equally sensitive to cleaning was not tested.
- The stemmer and stopword list were home-made because no package was installed. Different standard lists could differ, though the negation test suggests little effect on the cues.
- Quotes were selected from the transcripts ("only some participants are quoted"), and P8 and P9 have no quote. Counts are of quotes, not of participants' views, and say nothing about the full interviews or about customers generally.
- The technique cannot say which hypothesis is more true; it only shows the cue pattern is not an artefact of cleaning.

## Alternative explanations

- The stable cue counts may reflect how the quotes were selected, not how the participants felt. A reader who picked quotes to illustrate both sides would produce the same near-even split (four positive cue quotes, four negative cue quotes) regardless of cleaning.
- The two hang-up cues (P10, P5) may reflect scenario content rather than AI itself: Q7 ties P5's reaction to a car accident and Q8 ties P10's to a long introduction and an American accent. The pattern could be about the introduction and the voice, not about learning it is an AI.
- Q5 suggests the human-request cue may be a first impression that fades as the call proceeds, so "immediately" in H3 and H2 may be met for some participants even where a human was initially wanted.
