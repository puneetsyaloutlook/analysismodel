# Sensitivity of the voice AI interview conclusion to inclusion rules

## What was re-run

The favoured reading, from the eight result files taken together, is this. The interviews do not support H1 to H3 as a set. H1 is split and cannot be tested as a comparison with traditional voice automation. H2 is contradicted by P10 (unconditionally) and P5 (conditionally) in stated intention only. H3 is contradicted by P4 and P5 in stated intention only. Statements about hanging up and asking for a human cluster around the introduction. All of this describes patterns within this sample only.

The technique re-runs the tallies behind that reading while changing one choice at a time. Three tallies are used.

- H1: quoted participants who are favourable against those who are unfavourable about the voice AI, and whether any quote compares it with traditional voice automation.
- H2: quoted participants who say they would hang up.
- H3: quoted participants who say they would ask for a human.

Only six of the eight participants are quoted (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P10). P8 and P9 have no quote, so they are not counted anywhere except in one labelled bounding row.

The uncertain choices, written before re-running, are these: which participant to leave out; whether quotes or participants are the unit; whether hypothetical statements count; whether hedged statements ("probably", "if I was") count; whether P4's later approval cancels the early wish for a human; whether "immediately" requires a stated timing; whether H2 requires that the cause is learning it is an AI; how to code P1 and P4, who speak on both sides; whether context-specific complaints count against the AI; whether the comparator in H1 must be voice automation; and whether the text-cleaning choices in PE-02 change the cue counts.

Some of these choices weaken the favoured reading and some strengthen it. The rows that weaken it are included deliberately, because the favoured reading is the one being tested.

## Reading the table

Row 8, row 12 and row 21 are the most consequential for the favoured reading. They show that the statement "H2 and H3 are contradicted" depends on accepting hypothetical, sometimes hedged or conditional, statements as evidence, and on reading them as evidence about the AI and the moment of disclosure. Under the strictest rules the contradiction disappears and H2 and H3 become untested.

The same rows never produce support. No quote says a participant would stay on the line after learning it is an AI, and none says a participant would decline to ask for a human. The nearest case is P4 in Q5, whose early wish for a human fades, which is a change of mind about a process, not a statement about H3 as worded.

H1 moves between a tie (3 against 3) and 4 against 2 or 4 against 1, depending on how P1 and P4 are coded and on whether context-specific complaints count. It never reaches a clear favourable result on the comparison the hypothesis states, because no quote compares the AI with traditional voice automation directly (rows 0, 17).

Rows 1 to 6 show that no single participant decides the result. The H3 and H2 contradictions each fall to one participant when P4, P5 or P10 is left out, so each depends on two people.

## Does the conclusion reverse?

No row reverses the conclusion. No row produces explicit support for H1 as a comparison, for H2 or for H3, and the overall reading that the interviews do not support the three hypotheses as a set stays the same in all 21 changed rows.

Two parts of the favoured reading are less stable than the rest. The statement that H2 and H3 are contradicted weakens to "untested" under rows 8, 12 and 21, and rests on one participant each under rows 4, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11. The H1 split is a tie or a 4 against 2 lean, depending on rows 2 to 4, 14 and 15. The stable parts are that nothing affirms H2 or H3, that H1 cannot be tested against traditional voice automation, and that the hang-up and human-request statements are tied to the introduction and to the scenario.

## Assumptions and limitations

- Each row changes one rule, except row 21, which is combined and labelled. The rows are not exhaustive. Other defensible rules could be varied, such as weighting by quote length or by how explicit a statement is.
- The tallies use the codings from the eight result files, which were produced by single readers without a second coder. Rows 13 to 18 vary the codings that differ between the files, and other borderline codings (Q4, Q10 on H1) were not varied.
- The material gives no basis for choosing the ten quotes, and P8 and P9 have no quote. Counts are of selected quotes and of six participants. They are not rates, and no row can show how common any view is in the eight interviews or among customers.
- Every H2 and H3 statement is a hypothetical intention after listening to prerecorded scenarios. Row 8 shows the effect of excluding them, and no row can show behaviour in a live call.
- The material does not say whether or when the AI was disclosed, or which scenario each quote refers to apart from the motor claim reference in Q7. Rows 12 and 16 vary the readings but cannot supply these facts.
- Row 20 uses invented positions for P8 and P9 only to bound the result. It is not evidence.
- The technique tests how much the result depends on the choices made. It does not test whether the hypotheses are true.

## Alternative explanations

- The stability of the direction across rows may reflect how the quotes were selected. A selection that included strong reactions on both sides would produce a near-even split under almost any rule, so the robustness would describe the selection and not the interviews.
- Stated intentions about hanging up or asking for a human may follow the situation rather than the AI. P5's reactions are tied to a car accident (Q7), and P10's hang-up is tied to a long introduction (Q8). The result in rows 11, 12 and 16 is consistent with context and introduction design driving the pattern.
- The favourable quotes (Q1 to Q3) may reflect a low baseline from earlier chat bots and robot voices, not greater satisfaction than traditional voice automation. This would leave H1 unsupported even where the direction is favourable.
- Participants who spoke favourably may simply not have been asked about hanging up or requesting a human, so the empty cells for P1, P2 and P3 may reflect the questions or the quote selection and not a view.
