# Lens application: the article's claims sorted by the DeLone and McLean dimensions

## Material and conventions

The article has no item IDs, so items are referred to by the line number in /home/claude/sim_run/analysis_run/material/article.md (shown as L plus the number) and by IDs I assigned while listing (M, C, W and P). Counts below were produced by a script run over the table in this file.

## Step 1: items listed

Thirty-five items were listed: the measures the article says organisations use (M), the claims in its diagnosis (C), its five levels of workaround (W) and its four proposed measurement tiers (P). Long claims that bundle several ideas were split where the ideas fall in different categories (for example, L8 on what usage cannot show was split into C3 to C6).

## Step 2: lens chosen

The lens is the six dimensions of the updated DeLone and McLean model (E4): information quality, system quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction and net benefits. E4 states that use is separate from net benefits, that use and satisfaction influence net benefits, and that net benefits feed back into later use. I added a seventh row, "outside the lens", for claims that none of the six dimensions covers. Without it those claims would have been forced into the most common category, which is the failure this technique warns about.

## Step 3: category definitions

E4 names the dimensions but does not define them. The one-line definitions below are mine and are the standard reading of the names, so they are an assumption.

- Information quality: how accurate, complete and relevant the information or data the system holds or produces is.
- System quality: how well the system works as built, including fit to the task, integration, usability and speed.
- Service quality: the support around the system, such as training, help and accountable owners.
- Use: whether, how often and in what way people use the system.
- User satisfaction: people's response to the system, including trust and experience.
- Net benefits: the effect on work, customers, staff or the organisation, including cost and productivity.
- Outside the lens: claims about job design, incentives, analogy or the article's own reasoning.

## Step 6: reading the spread

**Where claims cluster.** Use and net benefits hold 20 of the 35 items. This matches the article's own framing, which is a contrast between counting use and measuring the benefit of the work. System quality is the third cluster (6 items), and it is where almost all of the workaround material sits (W1 to W3, C16, P1).

**Thin categories.** Information quality has one item (C4), service quality has one (W4) and user satisfaction has one (C5). None of the three has a measure attached. The article names data quality (L8) and trust (L8) as things usage cannot show, and names training (L26, L37) as a fix, but it proposes no way to measure any of them. Its proposed tiers contain no item whose primary category is information quality, service quality or user satisfaction.

**Claims without evidence of any kind.** No item in the table is supported in the article by data. The only statement about evidence is L29: "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation." No study, count, organisation or source is named. The claims that organisations report adoption (L4), that vendors price on seats and usage (L4) and that "Adoption seems to have gone mainstream" (L4) are also stated without support. The material cannot show whether the diagnosis describes common practice.

**What the sort says about the diagnosis.** The core claim, that use is not the same as net benefits, is consistent with E4, where use is a separate dimension that influences net benefits rather than standing in for it. The article is accurate in that narrow sense. The sort also shows that the article's "adoption" is only the use dimension. The other dimensions that the model treats as routine measures (user satisfaction, information quality, service quality) are not described as measured or as missing from existing programs. The article does not say whether organisations also collect them. The claim that adoption measures "cannot see workarounds" is argued by definition (L21) and by illustrations (L23 to L27), not by any count of what programs actually measure.

**What the sort says about the point of view.** The proposed replacement is weighted to net benefits (3 of 4 tiers) and system quality (1 of 4). The article's argument for abandoning use as a headline measure is reasoned, but two steps are not supported in the text. One is the step from "workaround present" to "design failed" (L31): the article states this as a general rule and does not consider workarounds that arise from other causes. L26 itself lists lack of training time, which is a service quality cause, and L27 lists an incentive cause. The other step is the claim at L17 that AI will become a component of everything, offered through the web search analogy only. The medium and longer tiers (L44, L45) depend on before and after comparison, which the article itself says is impossible for work that is removed (L9), so the tiers do not cover the category of value the article calls the largest gap. The article's last paragraph (L47) concedes the tiers are not new, which weakens the framing of the proposal as a workaround of its own.

## Borderline assignments

- C1 (L6) is about the relation between use and benefit. It is placed in use because it describes what use figures show, with net benefits as secondary.
- C5 (L8): trust in output could be read as information quality or user satisfaction. It is placed in user satisfaction. If it moved to information quality, that category would have two items and user satisfaction none.
- W4 (L26) is placed in service quality because the stated cause is training time. The team's behaviour is also a form of use, so a different rater could place it there.
- W5 (L27) and C12 (L15) are placed outside the lens. A rater who stretched use to cover the manager's management of recorded usage would move W5 to use, raising use to 11.
- M5 (L5) is placed in net benefits because it concerns the financial meaning given to usage. It could equally be use.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumed written category definitions. E4 supplies only the dimension names, so the definitions are mine and a different reader could define them differently.
- The sort depends on how I split the article into items. Splitting L8 into four items raised the counts for information quality, user satisfaction and system quality. Left as one item it would sit in one category and the thin categories would look thinner still.
- The article is one text with no stated date or publisher, and the page could not be fetched (L1). The material says nothing about what organisations actually measure.
- The technique shows which kinds of claim are present. It does not test whether any claim is true, and it cannot tell whether the missing categories are missing from the article or from the organisations it describes.
- I added a seventh category, which is outside the technique's usual form. Six items needed it.
- Counts were produced by a script over my own table, so they reflect my assignments and are not independent ratings. No second rater was available to test agreement, so the fit test (that two raters assign the same way) was not run.

## Alternative explanations

- The thin information quality, service quality and user satisfaction rows may reflect the article's purpose rather than a gap in its thinking. It is a short argument against one measure (use), so it would be expected to concentrate on use and net benefits. The absence of other dimensions could be scope, not omission.
- The system quality cluster may come from the article's own organising device (five levels of workaround) rather than from where the author's evidence lies. The structure produces six items there by construction.
- The absence of cited evidence may mean the article is an opinion piece written from practice, where the observations at L29 are the author's own. The material cannot distinguish an unreported study from an unsupported assertion.
- The weight on use and net benefits may be a feature of the lens. E4 gives use and net benefits their own dimensions, so an argument about adoption versus value falls into those two rows almost automatically.
