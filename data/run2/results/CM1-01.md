# Historical analogy: AI adoption measures and the IT productivity paradox

## Purpose and cases

The brief asks whether the article diagnoses its phenomenon accurately and whether its point of view is valid. This technique tests one part of that: whether the past case offered by the evidence folder (the productivity paradox of information technology, E3) is a fair guide to the present case, and what it does and does not tell us about the article's claims. The article itself does not cite the paradox. It reasons by its own analogies (ecommerce, the cloud, web search) and ends by saying "we've done this before with methods that still work". The comparison below is therefore my construction, not the article's argument.

Past case (E3): Brynjolfsson's 1993 review in Communications of the ACM. As recorded in the material, it "found that earlier studies generally did not identify significant productivity gains, and it judged that the evidence was inconclusive, partly because productivity from IT was almost impossible to assess with the measures then available." A later paper (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1998) "reported positive results from new data." E3 was read only as a search summary (abstract and introduction), so the mechanism behind the outcome is known at the level of "measures were inadequate", not in detail.

Present case, stated as the technique requires:

- Known (from the article): organisations report adoption (users, frequency, tokens) as progress. The article says this is because value "does not show up quickly in financial results". Vendor pricing and consultancy frameworks are said to follow adoption.
- Known (from E1 and E2, as evidence about the present only): E1 is a randomised trial of 16 experienced developers on 246 tasks. Tasks took 19% longer with AI allowed, yet developers afterwards believed AI had cut time by 20%. E2 is a Microsoft and LinkedIn survey: 78% of AI users bring their own AI tools to work, 39% have received company AI training, and 53% worry that revealing AI use signals they are replaceable.
- Unclear: how widespread workarounds are, how much value is of the "removes the need for the work" kind, and whether usage figures correlate with outcomes at all. The article gives no counts.
- Presumed by the article: that adoption is the main measure in use, that workarounds are common and invisible to usage data, and that the listed workarounds were observed. The article states "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation" but names no study, sample or date.

## Where the cases correspond (lessons that can be applied)

- Elements 1, 2 and 5. When a technology's benefit is not visible in outcome data, the cause may be partly the measures and partly a time lag. This supports the article's diagnosis that early reporting by adoption stands in for value that cannot yet be seen. It supports "partly", which is what E3 says.
- Element 4 (moderate). Better data later changed the finding in the past case, so an argument for improving the evidence is reasonable.

## Where the cases diverge (lessons that must not be applied)

- Elements 6, 9 and 11. E3 does not contain anything about workarounds, concealment or value from removed work. These parts of the article's diagnosis cannot be said to be supported by the past case. They rest on the article's reasoning and on the unnamed "actual observations".
- Element 3. The past measurement failure was too coarse an output measure. The present one, on the article's account, is a use measure standing in for outcomes. A fix suited to the first (more precise output data) is not the same as the article's fix (observe workarounds, then track outcomes).
- Element 12. The past case should not be used to claim that existing methods will resolve this quickly.

## What this means for the brief

On the part of the diagnosis that the past case can test (adoption or similar proxies reported because outcomes are hard to measure; measures are part of the problem; evidence improves later), the diagnosis is supported by analogy, though E3 was read only as a summary. On the part that is central to the article's point of view (workarounds hide behind usage figures, and the design is at fault), the analogy gives no support, and the evidence in the material is thin. E2's 78% bring-your-own-tools figure is consistent with the interface and task workaround of using a personal tool, but it is a vendor survey with unread method and it does not measure workarounds inside approved tools. E1 supports caution about perceived benefit but is narrow.

Claims the article makes without support in its own text: the statement that the listed workarounds are "actual observations from research" (no source), that adoption "seems to have gone mainstream" (no count), and that vendors and consultancies follow adoption (no examples). The article's reasoning is mostly sound where it is logical (use and effective work can diverge; a workaround can look like active use). It leaves out any estimate of how often these occur, any case where high adoption did coincide with good outcomes, and any alternative reason for non-improvement, such as a tool that is poorly suited to the task for reasons other than interface design, which it partly acknowledges when it lists data quality and trust.

## Assumptions and limitations

- Assumed that the productivity paradox is a reasonable past case. It was chosen by the brief, not tested against other candidates, so the fit test's concern about testing only one analogy applies. The result cannot say whether another case (for example earlier enterprise software rollouts, which the article mentions) would give different lessons.
- E3 was available only as a search summary of the abstract and introduction. The mechanism of the earlier outcome is therefore stated at a high level, and the 1998 reversal is one sentence with no detail on what changed.
- E1 and E2 were also search summaries. E2 is a vendor survey whose method was not read. E1 is a trial with 16 developers.
- The article gives no dates, publisher or sources, and the supplied text could not be checked against the original page.
- The technique compares topics; it cannot verify the article's claims about workarounds, which need observation data that the material does not hold.
- Ratings are my judgement on the material, not measured.

## Alternative explanations

- The strong correspondence on elements 1 and 2 may reflect only that any new technology has lagged and hard-to-measure benefits. If so, the paradox supports patience, not the article's specific view that adoption metrics are a distinct error.
- The article's diagnosis may be accurate in direction but its attribution of non-improvement to design failure may be too narrow. The same observed gaps between usage and results could come from tools that perform poorly (as E1 suggests for one task type), from tasks where AI does not help, or from lack of training (E2: 39% trained), none of which is a workaround.
- Adoption figures may be reported not because organisations cannot think of better measures but because they are cheap and early. That would make the issue one of incentives, not of an incorrect concept, and would weaken the article's claim that the problem lies in the basic concept of adoption.
