# Comparative case matrix: eight interviews against H1 to H3

## Cases, questions and selection

Each participant is one case. The three questions put to every case are taken directly from the hypotheses:

- H1: Does the participant report the voice AI as more satisfying than traditional voice automation (or similar earlier automation)?
- H2: Does the participant say they would hang up on learning it is an AI?
- H3: Does the participant say they would immediately ask for a human agent?

Selection reason: the material gives no reason why these eight were chosen or why only some were quoted. It says only that each participant heard the same four prerecorded scenarios. The cases are therefore treated as comparable on exposure, but the basis of selection and of quote selection is unknown. P8 and P9 have no quotes, so their rows are empty and are not counted as support or as contradiction.

## Consistent patterns

1. H1 has support from three cases and no direct counter from any case that compared the AI with earlier automation. P1 (Q1), P2 (Q2) and P3 (Q3) describe the AI as more dynamic, more human or more seamless. The only explicit comparison with earlier automation is in Q1, and it refers to chat bots, not voice automation. Q2 and Q3 compare implicitly, with "robot voice" and with having to "repeat yourself". So the link in this sample is between human-like, responsive interaction and a favourable view, but the material does not contain a direct comparison with traditional voice automation.
2. Where participants express reservations, the stated reasons differ by case but each is about the specific context or delivery, not about the AI being unable to do the task. These are the stressful setting (P5, Q7), an accent and AI-sounding voice (P10, Q4), an overlong introduction (P10, Q8) and past experience with AI agents (P1, Q6).
3. Every statement against H2 or H3 is a stated intention ("would"), not a described event. No quote reports that a participant actually hung up or actually asked for a human.

## Deviant cases

- P10 deviates on H2. Q8 is the only unconditional statement of hanging up, and it is tied to the length of the introduction.
- P5 deviates on H1, H2 and H3 together, but only for the motor claim scenario in Q7, and the hang-up in Q10 is conditional on not getting a human.
- P4 deviates on H3 (Q9), though Q5 shows the initial reluctance fading.
- P1 is internally mixed, supporting H1 in Q1 and expressing annoyance in Q6, so P1 does not sit cleanly on either side.

## What this means for the three hypotheses

- H1: partly supported within this sample, by P1, P2 and P3, and opposed by P5 and, less directly, P10. Four of the eight participants (P1, P2, P3, P5) give a clear position on satisfaction. The comparison with traditional voice automation is not tested directly in any quote.
- H2: not supported by P10 (Q8), and P5 (Q10) hedges it. No other participant speaks to hanging up. The material cannot show that most participants would stay on the line.
- H3: not supported by P5 (Q10) and in part by P4 (Q5, Q9). No other participant speaks to it, and P1's Q6 is ambiguous.

For a decision, the usable finding is a split: some participants praise the voice AI, while the participants who object (P4, P5, P10) all say they would ask for a human or hang up. The material shows no case supporting H2 or H3 by an explicit statement, only the absence of statements from the other cases.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumes the cases are comparable and that the same questions can be asked of each. Here the quotes were not elicited with a common question, so each cell is filled from whatever the participant happened to say. Most cells are "no evidence", which is not the same as "no".
- The technique requires a stated reason for selecting each case. The material gives none, and the quotes are described as a subset. If quotes were chosen to show striking reactions, the matrix over-represents strong views.
- P8 and P9 could not be assessed. Six of eight cases have any evidence, and for H2 and H3 only P4, P5 and P10 do.
- Scenarios differ across quotes. Only Q7 names a scenario (motor claim). Differences between participants may reflect different scenarios or different stages of the call.
- No counts beyond what the quote IDs show are possible, and nothing here generalises beyond this sample.

## Alternative explanations

- The split on H1 may reflect scenario and stress, not individual differences. The sceptical quote Q7 is explicitly about a car accident, whereas the favourable quotes (Q1 to Q3) do not name a scenario. The same participants might respond differently to a sales call.
- The H2 and H3 deviations may reflect first impressions of the introduction (Q8, Q9) more than a general rejection of AI. P4 (Q5) says the view changes as the process goes on, so hang-up and human-request intentions may be tied to the opening only.
- Prior experience may explain the pattern. P1 refers to earlier chat bots and AI agents (Q1, Q6), so P1's comparison may be shaped by non-voice systems, which would weaken the link to H1 as written.
- Hypothetical statements ("would") from people listening to prerecorded scenarios may not match behaviour in a real call, so the deviant cases may overstate or understate real hang-up and human-request behaviour.
