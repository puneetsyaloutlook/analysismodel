# Analysis of competing hypotheses: voice AI customer support interviews

## Hypotheses

The material gives three hypotheses. They are not mutually exclusive, so each is rated on its own against the evidence. Because the list may be incomplete, one rival hypothesis (H4) is added by the analyst. It is not in the material.

- H1: Customers find voice AI interactions more satisfying than traditional voice automation.
- H2: Customers do not hang up immediately when they learn they are speaking with an AI.
- H3: Customers do not immediately ask for a human agent.
- H4 (added): Reactions depend on the participant and the situation (for example stress, the voice used, the length of the introduction), so none of H1 to H3 describes the sample as a whole.

## Rating key

- C: consistent with the hypothesis
- I: inconsistent with the hypothesis
- N: neutral, not diagnostic, or the quote does not address it

## Notes on the ratings

- Q1 compares the AI with earlier chatbots, not with traditional voice automation, so its support for H1 is indirect. Q2 and Q3 contrast the AI with robot-sounding or repetitive systems, which is closer to H1 but still a reaction to one scenario.
- Q4 is rated weakly inconsistent with H1 because the stated concerns (AI sounding voice, American accent) are reasons for lower satisfaction. P10 does not say whether the interaction was satisfying overall.
- Q5 is rated consistent with H2 only weakly. P4 says the process improved over time, which suggests staying on the call, but it is a retrospective reaction to a prerecorded scenario.
- Q6 is ambiguous. The annoyance is linked to "previous experiences with AI agents", and it is not clear whether it refers to the scenarios heard. The remark about being offered a human at the beginning is not a request for one.
- Q10 is rated weakly inconsistent with H1 as a signal of dissatisfaction. The hang up in Q10 is conditional on not getting a human and follows a request, so it is not rated against H2.
- All of Q4 to Q10 are what participants say they would do. The material does not show any participant actually hanging up or asking for a human.

## Evidence that discriminates between hypotheses

- Q8 is the only item rated inconsistent with H2. It also counts against H1.
- Q5, Q9 and Q10 are rated inconsistent with H3. Q5 and Q9 come from P4 and Q10 from P5, so the inconsistency rests on two participants.
- Q7 and Q4 separate H1 from H4. They show negative reactions that H1 does not accommodate but H4 does.
- Q1, Q2 and Q3 favour H1 but do not distinguish H1 from H4, because H4 allows some participants to be satisfied.
- Q6 does not discriminate.

## Result

H4 has the fewest inconsistencies (none), but it is the least testable hypothesis, because it predicts that reactions vary and so it is consistent with almost any pattern. Among the three stated hypotheses, H2 has the fewest inconsistencies (one, Q8), but it also has the least evidence, with eight of ten items neutral. H3 has three inconsistencies from two participants (P4 and P5) and is the hypothesis the sample most clearly counts against. H1 has support from P1, P2 and P3 and counter-evidence from P4, P5 and P10, so the sample is split.

Within this sample, the interviews do not support H3, give mixed evidence on H1, and give little evidence on H2 either way (P10 is the single counter-case).

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumes each quote is an independent piece of evidence. Q5 and Q9 (P4), Q7 and Q10 (P5), Q4 and Q8 (P10) and Q1 and Q6 (P1) come from the same people, so the ten items represent fewer independent voices. Counts of C and I overstate the evidence base.
- Only some participants are quoted. P8 and P9 have no quotes, so the matrix cannot say anything about them. The material does not state whether the quotes were selected to illustrate particular views.
- The matrix cannot show how common each view is, because the material gives no complete record of what each participant said.
- Ratings of weak and neutral are judgements by the analyst. A different reader could rate Q1, Q5, Q6 and Q10 differently.
- H4 was added by the analyst to avoid an incomplete hypothesis list. It was not specified in the brief.
- The material cannot tell whether any participant actually learned they were speaking with an AI during a live call, since the scenarios were prerecorded. H2 and H3 are therefore tested only through stated intentions.
- "Traditional voice automation" is not described in the material, so H1 cannot be tested as a comparison.

## Alternative explanations

- Hypothetical scenario framing: the negative reactions in Q7 to Q10 are about imagined situations such as a car accident or a long introduction. Participants may predict stronger reactions than they would show in practice, which would weaken the case against H2 and H3.
- Scenario and voice effects: the difference between P1 to P3 (positive) and P4, P5 and P10 (negative or conditional) may reflect the scenario each person was thinking of (Q7 refers to a motor accident) or the voice (Q4, Q7) and not a general view of voice AI. This is the H4 reading.
- Introduction length as the main driver: Q8 and Q9 both point to the introduction, and Q6 mentions the offer of a human at the beginning. The pattern may be about the opening of the call, not about AI as such.
- Prior experience: Q1 and Q6 both mention earlier AI experiences, so satisfaction may depend on what each participant compared the scenarios with.
