# Competing hypotheses on the article's diagnosis and point of view

This result applies analysis of competing hypotheses (ACH) to the brief: is the article's diagnosis accurate and is its point of view valid. The article has no item IDs, so article items are labelled A1 to A7 (claims and arguments) and O1 and O2 (omissions). E1 (METR) and E2 (Microsoft and LinkedIn vendor survey) keep their evidence IDs. E2 shows what the vendor reports, not what is true of the world, and E1 does not independently confirm it, so E2 rows are not treated as confirmation.

## Hypotheses

- H1: The diagnosis is accurate and the article's claims are supported by its own evidence.
- H2: The diagnosis is plausible and partly supported, but the article asserts more than it shows (its evidence is illustrative and its causal and general claims are unsourced).
- H3: The phenomenon is real but the article places the problem wrongly. The main measurement problem is that usage overstates effect, and workarounds are secondary.
- H4: The premise is weak or not general: organisations already measure outcomes as well, so the article argues against a narrower practice than it describes.

Ratings: C is consistent, I is inconsistent, N is neutral or not applicable. Inconsistencies carry the weight, because consistent evidence rarely rules a hypothesis out.

## Evidence that discriminates

Items where at least one hypothesis is rated inconsistent and the ratings differ: A1, A2, A6, O1, O2, E2b, E2d.

- A1, A2, A6, O1 and O2 are inconsistent with H1 only. The article's central premise, its claim of observational research and its causal chain are stated without evidence, and it gives no data on how widespread adoption based measurement is. These items separate H1 from H2 and H3.
- E2d is inconsistent with H3 only. A vendor reported worry about revealing AI use suggests usage figures can understate use, which counts against the view that usage mainly overstates effect. The source is a vendor survey, so this is weak.
- E2b is inconsistent with H4 only. Leaders calling adoption critical fits an adoption focus, but it does not show adoption is how progress is measured, so it is a weak discriminator.

Items that do not discriminate: A3, A4, A5, A7, E1a, E1b, E2a, E2c. These are rated consistent or neutral everywhere. The internal logic (A3), the analogies (A4), the workaround taxonomy (A5), the concession in A7, and the E1 and E2 figures that match the article's examples all fit every hypothesis, so they should not move the conclusion. In particular E1a shows that use and effect can diverge, but it is software development only, and it is not about adoption metrics or workarounds.

## Result

H2 has the fewest inconsistencies (0), followed by H3 (1) and H4 (1), which tie. H1 has the most (5). On this material the article's diagnosis reads as plausible and its reasoning as internally sound, but its point of view is not supported by its own evidence to the standard its tone implies. The strongest points against it are the unsourced premise, the uncited claim of observational research, and the unshown causal chain from adoption figures to blaming people.

What the article assumes: that adoption reporting is the dominant practice, that workarounds are common enough to matter, and that fixing them raises value. What it leaves out: any count of organisations, any outcome from the proposed measures, and any evidence on the cost of observing work. It does state its own limit honestly in one place, by conceding that none of its tiers is new.

H2 beats H4 narrowly, and the margin depends on a single item (E2b) from a vendor source, so the ordering of H2, H3 and H4 is not firm. The separation of H1 from the others is firmer, because it rests on five article internal items.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The hypothesis list is mine and may be incomplete. H3 and H4 are the rivals I judged most plausible from the brief.
- Ratings are judgements about fit, not measurements. Another rater could move some C and N cells.
- Equal weight was given to every item, though items differ in strength. E2 items come from a vendor survey read only as a summary, and E1 was read only as a summary.
- The article could not be checked against any external population of organisations, so the premise that adoption is the dominant measure was tested only against what the article says and against E2b.
- H2 is rated consistent on every item, which makes it the hardest to refute and the broadest hypothesis. Its low count partly reflects that breadth. H4 is rated neutral on 9 items, so the material tells us little about it.
- Some inconsistencies for H1 are of one kind (missing evidence), so they are not five independent strikes.

## Alternative explanations

- The article may be a practitioner's argument from design experience, not a study. On that reading the missing sources are a genre choice, and H1 should be judged on plausibility and usefulness, not on citation.
- The inconsistencies for H1 may reflect the evidence supplied to this analysis (a summary of one trial and one vendor report) more than the article's real support. A fuller evidence base could shift the ratings.
- The ordering of H2 ahead of H4 may come from my choice of hypotheses. If H4 were framed as a claim about specific industries, it might rank differently.
