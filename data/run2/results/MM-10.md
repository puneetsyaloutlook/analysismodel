# Stance detection on the voice AI interview quotes

## Targets and label rules

The technique needs an explicit, defined target. The hypotheses give four, and each is labelled separately.

- T1: the voice AI scenarios as a customer support channel (general stance).
- T2: voice AI compared with older automation (chatbots, robot-sounding voice). This is the object of H1.
- T3: hanging up on realising it is an AI. This is the object of H2.
- T4: asking for a human agent at the start. This is the object of H3.

For T1 and T2, favour means the author speaks well of the target and oppose means the author speaks poorly of it. For T3 and T4, the target is a behaviour, so favour means the author says they would do it (or did), oppose means they say they would not, and neutral means they say both or leave it open. None means the quote does not mention the target. All T3 and T4 statements in the material are hypothetical ("would have"), not observed behaviour.

## Stance over time

The material does not date the quotes and says they are in no particular order. A time trend is not possible. The only change within a single speaker is P4 in Q5 (initial wish for a person, then "a really good process"), which is a shift within one interview, not a trend. P1 also holds both stances (Q1 favour, Q6 oppose) in different quotes, with no order given.

## What this says about H1 to H3 (patterns in this sample only)

- H1 (more satisfying than traditional automation): three of the eight participants (P1, P2, P3) favour voice AI against an older alternative, and none opposes it on that comparison. The link is limited. Only P2's comparator is voice, P1's is text chatbots, and P3's is implicit. Seven of ten quotes do not make the comparison. Four quotes (Q4, Q6, Q7, Q8, from P10, P1, P5, P10) oppose the AI scenarios in general, so satisfaction is split, not clearly favourable.
- H2 (do not hang up immediately on learning it is an AI): the stance labels run against H2 for two participants. P10 (Q8) and P5 (Q10) say they would have hung up, but P10 gives the intro length as the reason and P5 makes it conditional on no human being offered. Nobody reports hanging up because it was an AI. No quote supports H2 directly either, because staying on the line is not stated by any quoted participant.
- H3 (do not immediately ask for a human): the labels run against H3 for P4 (Q9) and P5 (Q10), who say they would ask for a human. P4's Q5 shows the wish fading as the call goes on, which fits "immediately" only partly. Seven of ten quotes are silent on the target.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumed an explicit target in each text. T1 holds in most quotes. T2 to T4 are mentioned in few quotes, so most rows are none (60% overall), which is a coverage gap in the material, not evidence of a neutral view.
- The technique's stated minimum is a defined target and usually 100 or more texts that mention it. This material has ten quotes from six of eight participants, and at most four mention any one target. The result describes these quotes only and cannot support any rate or percentage claim beyond the counts above.
- Participants P8 and P9 are not quoted, so the table cannot say whether they support or oppose anything. The quotes were selected by the compiler, and selection may over-represent strong reactions.
- Stance toward behaviours (T3, T4) was read from hypothetical statements. Nothing in the material records an actual hang-up or request for a human.
- Reported speech was handled by not labelling P1's Q6 mention of the human option as a request, and by treating the Q9 gloss as compiler text. The label for Q6 on T1 remains low confidence because the cause of the annoyance is unclear.
- The labels are one reader's judgement and were not checked by a second coder. Time and scenario breakdowns could not be produced.

## Alternative explanations

- The T4 and T3 favour labels may reflect general reluctance to deal with any automated system, or the length of the introduction (Q8), and not a reaction to the fact that it is an AI. The sample cannot separate these.
- The split on T1 may reflect the scenario heard (for example, P5's frustration in Q7 is tied to a car accident context) and not differences between customers. Participants who liked the AI (P2, P3) have no scenario recorded, so the context effect cannot be tested.
- P1 holding both stances (Q1 and Q6) suggests that stance may depend on which aspect is discussed (conversation quality versus intro or escalation options) and not on a fixed personal position.
- Favourable H1 statements may reflect comparison with a poor earlier experience (chatbots, robot voice) and not satisfaction with voice AI itself.
