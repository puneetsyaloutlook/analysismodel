# Definitional analysis of the key terms in the adoption and workaround article

## Scope

The brief asks whether the article accurately diagnoses a phenomenon (organisations measure AI transformation by adoption, and adoption measures miss workarounds and value) and whether its point of view is valid. Several terms in that question carry more than one meaning, in the question and in the article. This result replaces each with a working definition and tests it against borderline cases taken from the article's own examples. The material is one article, so no other source is used. The article has no numbered items, so passages are identified by section heading.

The technique needs at least two usages of each term. The article supplies two or more for every term below. The one exception is "evidence", where the article gives only one explicit statement, and this is noted in that entry.

## Glossary

### 1. Adoption

**Usages in the article**

- A counted quantity. Section "Adoption" opening paragraphs: adoption "counts use: how many people use an app or agent, how often they use its AI features, and how many tokens they consume".
- A stand-in for headcount reduction. "High usage suggests the same work could be done by fewer people".
- A normative state of effective work. Section "People use a tool as designed or work around it": "If adoption means anything at work, it means the tool works well enough, as designed, for people to get the job done with it."
- A claim about what people do. "AI adoption assumes that people actually "adopt AI". Taken literally, they do not."
- A coping device. "adoption is itself a workaround".

**Working definitions (the term needs two, kept apart)**

- Adoption (measured): the recorded use of a tool or agent that contains AI, such as active licences, session counts, feature calls or tokens consumed, over a period. This is what the article attacks as a measure.
- Adoption (effective): people completing their work with the tool in the way it was designed, with the tool being good enough for that. This is the article's own standard in the third usage above.

**Why two definitions are needed.** The article's central claim is that measured adoption misses workarounds. Under the definition of effective adoption, a workaround is by definition the opposite of adoption, so the claim that adoption measures fail to see workarounds is partly true by definition. What is empirically open is how often measured adoption and effective adoption diverge, and the article gives no figure for that.

**Borderline cases**

| Case (from the article) | Measured adoption | Effective adoption |
|---|---|---|
| A person needing ten attempts, then finishing by hand | High (more tokens) | No |
| A team running old and new process in parallel | Full | No |
| A person using a tool with AI built in, unaware of which parts use AI | Counted if feature calls are logged, otherwise unclear | Yes, if used as designed, but the article says this is "not adopting AI" |
| A person who "learns a new job" reviewing agent exceptions | Counted only as use of the review tool | Unclear, because the article says usage figures cannot answer whether the job is well designed |
| A person using a personal tool because the approved tool is slow | Low for the approved tool | No for the approved tool, but possibly yes for AI generally |

**Where answers differ by definition:** the parallel-running team, the ten-attempt user and the personal-tool user. The first two are adopters under one definition and not under the other. The third is a non-adopter of the approved tool and a possible adopter of AI, so the answer depends on whether the unit is the tool or the technology.

### 2. AI transformation

**Usages in the article**

- Title: "a workaround for measuring AI transformation", which treats it as a programme with progress to be measured.
- Section "What an AI program should measure and do instead": an "end state", described as "the future it set out to reach", with redesigned work and "work that is no longer needed".
- Opening paragraph: enterprises "getting value from the AI-based solutions they deploy", which treats it as deployment of solutions.

**Working definition:** a programme of deliberate change to how work is done or what is delivered, in which AI is part of the means, judged by changes in work outcomes and not by the presence of AI.

**Why this definition.** The article's own position (AI becoming "a component of everything") implies that transformation cannot be defined as "increase in AI use". The definition therefore excludes use as a criterion and includes redesign of work.

**Borderline cases**

- Rolling out an email assistant to all staff with no change to any process. Deployment, but not transformation under this definition.
- The article's examples of work removed (documents that update automatically; a product built so an error cannot happen). Transformation under this definition, though the article says nothing in them "is being used more than it was before".
- A speed-up of an existing step. The article treats this as measurable value. Whether it is transformation or only improvement depends on whether the work's structure changed.

**Where answers differ by definition:** the email assistant and the speed-up. A deployment-based definition counts both. The definition above counts neither, and this changes whether the article's phenomenon (measuring transformation by adoption) is a measurement error or a category error about what is being measured.

### 3. Workaround

**Usages in the article**

- "they find a workaround that gets the job done" (as the alternative to using a tool as designed).
- Five levels, each with examples: interface, task, workflow, team, leadership.
- "adoption is itself a workaround", where the word is applied to a measurement practice.

**Working definition:** an action by a person that achieves a work outcome by a route other than the one the tool or process was designed for, because the designed route did not let them achieve it (or did not seem to). The article's causal clause ("because they are responsible for getting the work done and the tool as designed did not let them do it") is part of the definition it uses, so the working definition keeps it.

**Test against the article's own examples.** Not every example meets the definition.

| Example | Meets the definition? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Interface: other applications kept open beside the tool | Yes | Designed route insufficient, person compensates |
| Interface: personal tool used instead of the slow approved tool | Yes, but also a policy breach | The route is non-designed, and it moves AI use outside the measured tool |
| Task: staff change records so the agent skips the review | Borderline | It achieves the outcome by a non-designed route, but it removes a control, and the article does not say the review was needed or not |
| Workflow: rebuilding lost context from other tools | Yes | Clear compensation for a design gap |
| Team: opening the tool daily to record usage, then working the old way | Borderline | The workaround is of the measure and the training requirement, not of the tool's function |
| Leadership: keeping usage high enough to comply but low enough not to imply headcount cuts | Borderline | This manages the measure and its consequences. It does not route work around a tool |

**Where answers differ by definition:** the last three rows. If a workaround must achieve the work by another route, the team and leadership examples are gaming of a measure and fall outside the term. If a workaround is any behaviour that departs from designed use for a reason connected to the design or programme, they fall inside. This matters for the claim that "each workaround shows where the design failed": for the team and leadership cases, the failure shown is of the measurement and incentive arrangements, and not of tool design. The article's fix for those levels (time for training, honest reporting) is consistent with this, but its heading "Each workaround shows where the design failed" is wider than what these two examples support.

**A second borderline.** The article says a person "does one of two things": uses the tool as designed or finds a workaround. Partial use, mixed use and abandonment fit neither. Abandonment would appear in usage figures as low use, which is the case adoption measures do see. The two-way division therefore narrows the set of cases to those that favour the article's argument.

### 4. Value (and "value that adoption cannot see")

**Usages in the article**

- Value as board and executive outcomes: "cost reduction and customer service" and "productivity gains".
- Value as measurable improvement: "the same work now takes less time and can be compared with last year's figures".
- Value as work removed: "They remove the need for it."
- Value as the purpose of investment: whether work "is done better, faster or more reliably, which is what the original investment was for".

**Working definition:** a change in an outcome the organisation or its customers care about (cost, time, quality, error rate, experience), measured against a stated earlier state or counterfactual.

**Why a counterfactual is part of the definition.** The article says of removed work that "there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with". Under a comparison-based definition, removed work has value that cannot be measured by comparison, which is the article's point. Under a definition without a baseline, the value can be stated as a saved cost.

**Borderline cases**

- Removal of requests and follow-up calls (the document example). Real value, no baseline in the work itself, though a baseline exists in the earlier volume of requests, which the article does not mention.
- The error that "cannot happen". The checking team's earlier workload is a possible baseline.
- Workarounds found and removed, offered as a short-term measure. Whether this is value or only a process indicator depends on the definition, since it measures the removal of friction and not a work outcome.

**Where answers differ by definition:** the first two cases. The article says adoption "has no way to record this value". That is true of usage counts. It does not follow that the value cannot be measured at all, and the article's own medium-term tier tracks "time, quality, errors" against "how it was done before", which would apply to these cases. The article does not reconcile this with its statement that no earlier version exists.

### 5. Measure (and "adoption measure")

**Usages in the article**

- Usage counts reported as progress ("AI programs report adoption instead").
- The proposed tiers, which include observation and conversation ("Observe the work as it is done, ask people what they do").
- "Leadership workarounds need measures based on the outcomes of the work rather than on usage".

**Working definition:** a repeatable procedure that yields a comparable record of some property of the work or the tool, which can be reported to a programme over time. This includes qualitative records if the procedure is repeatable.

**Borderline cases**

- A count of "workarounds found and removed". It is countable, but its meaning depends on how thoroughly workarounds are looked for.
- Observation and interviews. Repeatable if structured, otherwise not.
- Token consumption. Countable, but the article uses it as a bad measure of effective work, while it also has a legitimate role in cost.

**Where answers differ by definition:** observation and interviews. If a measure must be numeric and comparable, the article's short-term tier is not a measure and the alternative it proposes to adoption is weaker than presented. If qualitative records count, the tier is a measure but is subject to the same question of how many are found, which is the weakness the article attributes to adoption counts.

### 6. Evidence (as in "supported by its own evidence")

**Usages in the article**

- "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation." This is the only statement about evidence for the five workaround levels.
- Statements about what boards, executives, vendors and consultancies do, given without any source.

The article gives one explicit evidence statement and one implicit pattern (unsourced claims of fact). The minimum of two usages is met only loosely.

**Working definition (for this analysis):** material in the article that a reader could check, meaning a named study, dataset, count or observation with an identifiable source, or an argument whose premises are stated and whose conclusion follows from them.

**Borderline cases**

- The workaround examples. The article says they are from research, but names no study, sample or setting. A reader cannot check them. They are usable as illustrations of a mechanism, not as evidence of frequency.
- The web search analogy. It is a historical example offered in support of the claim that AI will become an ordinary component. It is a single case.
- The statement that "none of these tiers is new". An argument from established practice, with no examples named.

**Where answers differ by definition:** the workaround examples. If evidence includes plausible illustrations of a mechanism, the article's claim that workarounds exist at every level is supported. If evidence requires a checkable source, the claim of frequency is unsupported, and the article's claim about the phenomenon (that organisations measure transformation by adoption) is also unsupported beyond its stated assertion, since no organisation, survey or example is cited for it.

## What the definitions show about the brief's question

1. The phenomenon, as defined above, has two parts with different support. That organisations report adoption is asserted in the first paragraph with no source. That adoption counts cannot distinguish designed use from workaround is supported by the logic of the definition of measured adoption (a count of use does not record how the use happened) and does not need empirical evidence.
2. The claim that adoption measures "miss workarounds" is largely true by definition once effective adoption is defined as the absence of workarounds. The article does not state which definition it is using at each point. This is the main definitional weakness.
3. The claim that adoption measures miss value is true for removed work only if "value" requires a baseline within the work itself. The article's own medium-term tier implies a baseline can be built, which is in tension with its statement about removed work.
4. The workaround category is wider than the article's heading suggests. Two of five levels (team and leadership) are behaviours aimed at the measure, so they show a failure of measurement and incentives and not of tool design.
5. The two-way division of behaviour (designed use or workaround) leaves out abandonment, partial use and legitimate use of AI outside the approved tool, and these are cases that usage counts would partly capture.
6. What the article leaves out, as shown by the borderline cases: any figure for how often adoption counts and effective use diverge; any case where high adoption does reflect effective use; any organisation that does measure by outcome already; and how the proposed tiers avoid the same problem of being reported as a number without meaning.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumed that the article's terms can be given stable definitions from the article's own usages. Where the article uses one term in several senses (adoption, workaround), the result keeps both senses and does not choose between them for the author.
- The working definitions are mine and are tested only against the article's examples. Two analysts might draw the lines differently, particularly for "workaround" and "evidence".
- The technique could not assess whether the phenomenon is real. It clarifies what would count as the phenomenon. The article names no organisation, survey or study, and the material holds nothing to check the claims about boards, executives or vendors.
- The technique did not count anything in the text. No code was used, because the technique does not call for counting or text comparison.
- Quotations were selected from the article as supplied. The material states that the page could not be fetched and the text was supplied by the user, so it may differ from the published version. The date and publisher are not given.
- The term "point of view valid" was not given its own glossary entry, because its content is covered by the entries for adoption, workaround, value, measure and evidence.

## Alternative explanations

- The article may mean "adoption" in one consistent sense, the narrow counted sense, and use the normative sentence ("If adoption means anything at work...") only as a rhetorical contrast. On that reading there is no equivocation, and the claim that counts miss workarounds is a straightforward point about what counts record. The definitional tension I found would then be a matter of presentation, not of reasoning.
- The wider workaround category may be intended. The article may treat gaming of a measure as a workaround of the programme, so that team and leadership examples belong in the term by design. On that reading the heading "Each workaround shows where the design failed" refers to the design of the programme and its incentives, not only of the tools, and the mismatch I found is a matter of wording.
- The apparent conflict between "no earlier version" and the medium-term comparison with "how it was done before" may be a difference of scale. The first refers to a single removed task, the second to the whole piece of work the programme changes. On that reading the two statements are consistent.
- The absence of cited evidence may reflect the form (a short opinion article on a blog platform) and not a weakness in the underlying research the article says it draws on.
