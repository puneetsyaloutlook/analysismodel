# Key assumptions check on the article's diagnosis and point of view

## Scope and method

The brief asks whether the article accurately diagnoses its phenomenon (organisations measure AI transformation by adoption, and adoption measures miss workarounds and value) and whether its point of view is valid. The favoured reading, taken from the seven earlier results, is this: the narrow diagnosis follows from how a usage count works; the claims about how common the phenomenon is are asserted and not shown; and the wider conclusions (that every workaround shows design failure, that nobody adopts AI, that tiered reporting is the fix) are less well supported than the narrow diagnosis.

This check lists the premises that the favoured reading needs to be true, in two groups. Part A covers the article's own premises. Part B covers the premises inside the earlier findings, because a check of the findings has to test what they take for granted as well as what the article does. Each premise is marked stated or unstated, and challenged, then kept or dropped. Each kept premise has the conditions that would show it false. The article gives almost no conditions of its own, so the conditions below are my constructions from the material and the findings, and they are labelled as such.

The article has no item numbers. Quotations are copied exactly from the article. The material is one article of unknown date and publisher, supplied as text, so nothing outside the article was checked. Findings that mention E1, E2, E3 and E4 are cited as the earlier results reported them, and I could not check those summaries.

## Part A: premises in the article

### A1. Organisations mostly report adoption as their measure of AI progress

- Origin: stated, with one hedge. "Adoption seems to have gone mainstream as the way organisations describe their progress with AI".
- Challenge: this is the factual premise of the whole diagnosis. No organisation, survey or count supports it, and the article does not say what share of programs also report outcome measures.
- Status: kept, marked vulnerable.
- Invalidating condition (mine): evidence that most programs report outcome or quality measures next to usage, in which case the article argues against a narrower practice than it describes.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A2. Programs report adoption because it can be counted early, and vendors and consultancies follow

- Origin: stated. "So AI programs report adoption instead, because it can be counted from the first week of a rollout." Also "Following their lead, vendors set their pricing based on seats and usage, and consultancies build their frameworks around it."
- Challenge: the first sentence names one cause. A second cause, that seat and usage data come with the vendor's product and cost nothing to collect, fits the text equally well and reverses the direction of influence. The article does not choose between them.
- Status: kept for the claim that adoption is cheap and early; dropped for the claim about who leads whom, because nothing supports it and the diagnosis does not need it.
- Invalidating condition (mine): evidence that vendor reporting tools came first and programs adopted what was supplied.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A3. A count of use cannot tell designed use from a workaround

- Origin: stated. "A workaround does not show up as non-use."
- Challenge: this holds for the usage figures the article defines (people using an app, frequency of AI feature use, tokens consumed). It does not hold automatically for every dashboard a real organisation runs. The earlier results accept it as true by logic. That is correct for the article's definition and untested for real telemetry, which may include completion, error or support-contact data.
- Status: kept. This is the best supported premise in the article.
- Invalidating condition (mine): usage reporting that is routinely read together with task completion, time per task or support requests, so that workarounds leave a visible trace.
- Stated invalidating condition: none. The article does not consider richer usage data.

### A4. Workarounds are common enough, and large enough in effect, to make adoption figures misleading

- Origin: unstated. The article gives examples of workarounds at five levels and no frequency for any.
- Challenge: the diagnosis needs workarounds to be frequent, because a rare behaviour would not make a count misleading. The article shows that workarounds can exist. It does not show that they are widespread.
- Status: kept as needed for the diagnosis, marked vulnerable, and named below as a likely point of error.
- Invalidating condition (mine): observation across several programs in which workarounds are rare, or in which usage figures track outcomes closely.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A5. The workaround examples were observed

- Origin: stated by assertion. "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation."
- Challenge: no study, sample, setting or date is named. The five-level typology and the task example about editing records depend on this one sentence. It cannot be confirmed or refuted from the material.
- Status: kept provisionally, as an illustration of a mechanism and not as evidence of frequency.
- Invalidating condition (mine): the research does not exist, or the examples came from a single setting that is atypical.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A6. A person either uses a tool as designed or works around it

- Origin: stated. "Faced with any tool at work, a person does one of two things. They use it as designed, or they find a workaround that gets the job done."
- Challenge: the split leaves out partial use, mixed use, plain non-use and abandonment. It matters because abandonment is the case usage counts do see, so the split narrows the cases to those that favour the argument.
- Status: dropped in its absolute form. Kept as a simplification for the typology, with the consequence that the diagnosis covers the cases where use is recorded and the work is done another way, and not all poor outcomes.
- Invalidating condition (mine): any observed pattern that is neither designed use nor a successful workaround, which is easy to find.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A7. Adoption, properly understood, means effective use as designed

- Origin: stated. "If adoption means anything at work, it means the tool works well enough, as designed, for people to get the job done with it. A workaround is its opposite."
- Challenge: with this definition, adoption excludes workarounds by construction, so "adoption measures miss workarounds" is partly true by definition. The earlier results say this in two places (FQ-03 and TC1-21) and count it both as support and as a defect. It is neither on its own. It makes the narrow claim safe from refutation and moves the empirical question to A4.
- Status: kept as a stipulation the author is entitled to make, with the cost that the claim then says less about the world than it appears to.
- Invalidating condition (mine): the article uses "adoption" in the counting sense elsewhere in the same argument without saying so, which it does ("adoption is itself a workaround", and the title).
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A8. Adoption programs depend on a literal meaning of "adopt AI"

- Origin: stated. "AI adoption assumes that people actually "adopt AI". Taken literally, they do not."
- Challenge: the article itself describes adoption as a habit "borrowed from earlier software rollouts, where success meant getting people to use the new system", which is an ordinary organisational sense and not a literal one. Nothing in the diagnosis needs the literal reading.
- Status: dropped as a premise of the diagnosis. The earlier results differ on how much this matters: TC1-19 calls Argument 4 the weakest link, and FQ-03 says the article's point "stands without this step". Both are correct. The premise is not needed for the diagnosis, and it is needed only for the recommendation to stop organising programs around adopting AI.
- Invalidating condition (mine): programs that use the word loosely, which the article concedes.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A9. AI is becoming a component of every tool, as web search did

- Origin: stated as present fact. "A program built around adopting AI is organised around a technology that is becoming a component of everything."
- Challenge: this is a prediction supported by one past case, and it covers only the article's first case (a tool with AI inside). The second case, a person who learns a new job because agents do part of the work, is not a component becoming ordinary. The article also says "AI is not a single thing that a person chooses and takes responsibility for", and gives no ground for that against standalone assistants that people do choose and answer for.
- Status: kept, marked vulnerable.
- Invalidating condition (mine): AI products that stay separate and are chosen deliberately by staff, which is the way many organisations currently buy them.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A10. Every workaround shows where the design failed

- Origin: stated. "Each workaround shows which part of the design failed, for whom, and what people do instead."
- Challenge: the article's own team example (no time to train) and leadership example (a manager shaping recorded usage) come from workload and incentives, and its remedies for them are training time and a statement on how figures will be used. The claim is secure for interface, task and workflow cases. The article gives one conditional limit: "Training people to use the tool as designed will not help when the design is the reason they work around it." That limit is about training and does not extend the claim to the other causes.
- Status: kept only in a wider form ("design of the tool, the work and the incentives"), which the article does not state. Dropped as written.
- Invalidating condition (mine): any workaround traced to skill, preference or an unsafe shortcut and not to a design fault.
- Stated invalidating condition: partial (the training limit above).

### A11. Programs blame people because they have no evidence about design

- Origin: stated. "A program measured by adoption rates cannot see workarounds, so when the work does not improve it has no evidence about the design and looks for the cause in people instead."
- Challenge: this is a causal story about program behaviour with no case behind it. It also treats "the organisation did not follow the program" as a claim about people, when it may be a claim about the program. Other causes of poor results, such as a tool that performs badly or a task that AI does not help, are not considered.
- Status: kept as plausible, marked vulnerable.
- Invalidating condition (mine): programs that measure by adoption and still examine design through support tickets, complaints or user research.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A12. Removed work cannot be recorded by an adoption measure, and this is the largest gap

- Origin: stated. "An adoption measure has no way to record this value." and "The largest gap is value that adoption cannot see at all."
- Challenge: the first claim follows for usage counts. The second is a comparison supported by two hypothetical examples. The article says "there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with", yet its medium-term tier compares against "how it was done before". A baseline for removed work may exist in the earlier volume of requests or checks, which the article does not mention. The earlier results disagree on whether this is a contradiction or a difference of scale (TC1-19 offers the scale reading as an alternative). The text supports either reading.
- Status: kept for usage counts. Dropped as "cannot be measured at all", and unproven as "largest".
- Invalidating condition (mine): an organisation that records the earlier volume of the removed work and shows the change.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A13. A count of workarounds found and removed is a valid early sign of value

- Origin: unstated. The text says only "Workarounds found and removed can be reported from the first weeks of a rollout, which is when adoption figures are reported now."
- Challenge: this is the step that links the diagnosis to the remedy. Fewer workarounds could mean a better tool, or staff who have learned not to show them, which is the pattern the article describes for usage figures. The article proposes a new measure that is easy to report early, after faulting adoption for that quality.
- Status: kept as needed for the remedy, marked vulnerable, and named below as a likely point of error.
- Invalidating condition (mine): a program in which workaround counts fell while outcomes did not improve.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A14. Observation and questioning will reveal workarounds

- Origin: unstated.
- Challenge: the team and leadership examples describe people hiding or shaping their use, so candour cannot be assumed. The leadership fix (a statement of how figures will be used) addresses this, but the short-term tier does not say how observation avoids the same distortion. The cost of observing work is also not discussed, while the article says adoption is used because it "can be counted from the first week".
- Status: kept, marked vulnerable.
- Invalidating condition (mine): observers finding fewer workarounds where staff fear consequences than where they do not.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A15. People who work around a tool have judged the situation correctly

- Origin: unstated. It sits behind the advice that task workarounds need "checks that ask for real judgement, not approval", which follows the example of staff editing records so a review does not trigger.
- Challenge: "responsible for getting the work done" does not show that they were right to skip the review. The example could be a poor review step or a removed control, and the article does not say how to tell them apart.
- Status: kept, marked vulnerable.
- Invalidating condition (mine): a case where the skipped review would have caught an error.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

### A16. Established methods will work for this kind of change

- Origin: stated. "These principles were true before AI and are still true now."
- Challenge: the article also says AI is changing work "in ways that are hard to picture in advance". TC1-13 notes that the one historical case it examined was inconclusive for years and gives no support for expecting quick results from older methods. The appeal rests on the claim being obvious.
- Status: kept as a reason the proposal is low risk, dropped as a reason it will work.
- Invalidating condition (mine): a type of AI-driven change for which observation, outcome design and role design have been tried and failed.
- Stated invalidating condition: none.

## Part B: premises inside the earlier findings

### B1. The narrow diagnosis is sound because it follows from logic

- Origin: unstated in the findings, but common to FQ-03, TC1-19, TC1-21, DM-07 and CM1-14.
- Challenge: this is A3 and A7 restated. The soundness depends on the article's own narrow definitions of usage figures and of adoption. Under those definitions it is close to true by construction. Under a looser definition of either term, it becomes an empirical claim that the material cannot test.
- Status: kept, with that dependence added to the favoured reading.
- Invalidating condition: either definition changes, or real reporting practice is shown to differ from the article's description.

### B2. Agreement across the findings is independent confirmation

- Origin: unstated.
- Challenge: the seven results reach the same few conclusions (unsourced premise, team and leadership examples not design faults, tension between removed work and the medium-term tier, no evidence for the tiers). They were all produced from the same article, and several use each other's readings of the same passages. The repeated points are closer to one observation seen from several angles than to seven separate tests. The same applies to the five inconsistencies counted against H1 in DM-07, which that result itself says are of one kind.
- Status: kept with reduced weight. The agreement shows the points are visible in the text. It does not show they are correct.
- Invalidating condition: the same conclusions can be reached by a method that does not use the article's wording, which none of the seven results did.

### B3. Missing sources count against the article

- Origin: unstated, and handled in two ways. Every result lists the absence of sources as a defect, and every result also lists genre (a short opinion piece) as an alternative explanation.
- Challenge: the brief asks whether claims are supported by the article's "own evidence", so absence of support is a fair finding whatever the genre. Absence of support does not show a claim is false. The findings sometimes slide from "unsupported" to "doubtful".
- Status: kept in the form "unsupported, not shown wrong".
- Invalidating condition: the research cited in the sentence "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation." is produced and bears out the examples.

### B4. H2 is the best reading because it has the fewest inconsistencies

- Origin: stated in DM-07.
- Challenge: DM-07 itself says H2 is rated consistent on every item, which makes it the broadest and hardest to refute. "Partly supported, but asserts more than it shows" would fit nearly any opinion article. Its zero count reflects its breadth as much as its fit. The firm result in that matrix is only that H1 (fully supported) is not credible, and that rests on missing-evidence items.
- Status: kept as a modest reading. It is not strong evidence that the article is more or less accurate than average.
- Invalidating condition: a narrower version of H2 that makes a testable claim, such as a stated share of workarounds, and is scored against the material.

### B5. Evidence from outside the article bears on the article's own evidence

- Origin: unstated. TC1-13 and DM-07 use E1 (a trial) and E2 (a vendor survey), and TC1-13 says E3 and E4 were read only as summaries.
- Challenge: E1 and E2 are not cited by the article, so they cannot show that the article is supported by its own evidence. Both findings say as much, and E2 is rated by DM-07 as a vendor survey that does not confirm anything. Even so, the results use them to colour conclusions, such as the 78% figure that "is consistent with" the personal-tool example. I could not see these sources and have not relied on them.
- Status: kept only as context. Dropped as support for or against the article's own claims.
- Invalidating condition: the sources are read in full and found to measure the behaviours the article describes.

### B6. The team and leadership examples fall outside "design failure"

- Origin: stated in FQ-03, TC1-19, TC1-21, CM1-01 and CM1-14.
- Challenge: this depends on reading "design" as tool design. The article's own workflow experience idea covers which steps are automated, what passes at each handover and who is accountable, which is wider. FQ-03 and TC1-19 both give the wider reading as an alternative. The article's heading is ambiguous and does not state which it means.
- Status: kept as a finding about wording, dropped as a finding about reasoning. The article should say which meaning it intends.
- Invalidating condition: the author states that "design" includes workload and incentives.

## Likely points of error

These are the kept premises for which the article states no condition that would show them false, and which the diagnosis or the remedy needs. They are listed in order of how much of the argument depends on them.

1. A1, that organisations mostly report adoption. The whole diagnosis starts here and no support is given.
2. A4, that workarounds are common enough to matter. The article proves existence and not frequency.
3. A13, that a count of workarounds found and removed is a valid early sign of value. This is the unstated link between diagnosis and remedy, and it is open to the concealment problem the article raises itself.
4. A14, that observation will reveal workarounds in settings where the article says people hide them.
5. A10 and A11, that every workaround is a design failure and that programs blame people for lack of design evidence. Both are causal claims with no case behind them, and the article's own examples go against the first.
6. A9, that AI is becoming a component of everything. It is a prediction stated as present fact.

Premises that are safer: A3 (a usage count does not record how work was done) and A7 as a stipulation. Their safety comes from definitions, so they say less about practice than they appear to.

## What this means for the favoured reading

The favoured reading survives with three changes. First, the narrow diagnosis is sound mainly because the article defines usage figures and adoption in a way that makes it so. Second, the seven results agree more than they independently confirm, so the weight of the agreement should be lowered. Third, the weakest part of the point of view is the remedy, because the unstated premises A13 and A14 connect it to the diagnosis and the article gives no condition under which they would fail. The article's claims about prevalence are unsupported and should be treated as untested, not as wrong.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The premise list is my reconstruction. A different reader could split the article's claims differently, for example treating A10 and A11 as one premise.
- Marking a premise "vulnerable" is a judgement about how much support the material gives it. It is not a measure of how likely the premise is to be false.
- The invalidating conditions are mine, because the article states almost none. They are plausible tests and not tests the author accepts.
- Part B depends on reading the seven earlier results as written. I did not re-run their methods, and I could not check E1 to E4, which they cite as summaries.
- The material is one article with no source, date or publisher, and it was supplied as text, so it may differ from the published page.
- This check cannot say whether the phenomenon is real. It lists what must be true for the favoured reading to hold.
- No counting or code was needed for this technique.

## Alternative explanations

- The article may be written as a practitioner's framework and not as a study. On that reading the missing sources and conditions are a choice of form, and the unstated premises (A4, A13, A14) are the author's experience, which the reader is asked to test against their own. The material cannot tell an unreported study from an unsupported assertion.
- The wide use of "design" and "adoption" may be deliberate. If the author means design to include workload and incentives, and adoption to mean effective use, then A7, A10 and B6 are consistent, and the problems found are problems of stated definitions and not of reasoning.
- The tension between removed work and the medium-term comparison may be sequencing and scale. The article may intend removed work to be handled only by the end-state tier, which would make A12 consistent with the remedy.
- The agreement across the findings (B2) may reflect that the article has a small number of clear weaknesses, so several methods reach the same ones. On that reading the agreement is more informative than I have allowed.
- The favoured reading may be too cautious. If the readers the article is written for already know that workarounds are common, the narrow diagnosis needs no frequency evidence, and the gaps in A1 and A4 matter less.
