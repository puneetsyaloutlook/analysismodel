# Voice AI interviews: four themes read against H1 to H3

Reflexive thematic analysis of the ten quotes (Q1 to Q10). Only six of the eight participants are quoted (P1, P2, P3, P4, P5, P10). P8 and P9 have no extracts in the material, so nothing is said about them. The themes are patterns of meaning in this sample only.

## Theme 1: Fluency and human-likeness are what earns approval

**Description.** Favourable reactions are explained by how the AI compares with something worse the participant has already met. The AI is judged by whether it sounds like a person, asks the right questions and spares the caller repetition. The reference point differs: a keyword-matching chatbot, a robotic voice, or the experience of repeating oneself.

**Data extracts.**
- Q1 (P1): "This AI definitely seems a bit more, uh, advanced." P1 contrasts it with earlier chatbots that "just parsed, um, articles from their own website" and concludes "this one seems to be pretty dynamic."
- Q2 (P2): "It seemed like talking to a real person." P2 adds that it "knew exactly what it needed to ask."
- Q3 (P3): "that was really excellent. It was like really seamless." P3 contrasts it with having "to repeat yourself and stuff."

## Theme 2: Initial resistance driven by prior experience, which may soften

**Description.** Some participants arrive wary because of earlier encounters with AI agents. For P4 the wariness is described as lifting as the interaction proceeds. For P1 it shows as annoyance, and the remedy P1 mentions is an early offer of a human.

**Data extracts.**
- Q5 (P4): "at first. I mean, if it was myself, I'd be probably wanting to speak to someone, but then as the process goes on, you realise it's actually, um, a really good process."
- Q6 (P1): "I definitely felt, um. I felt a little bit annoyed 'cause I've had pre uh, previous experiences with AI agents especially..."

## Theme 3: Voice quality, setting and length decide tolerance

**Description.** Negative reactions point at specific features of the scenario rather than at AI in general: an American accent, a voice heard as cold, a stressful situation such as a car accident, and an introduction that runs too long. These participants judge the AI against what the moment calls for (local familiarity, warmth, speed).

**Data extracts.**
- Q4 (P10): "First of all, this sounds very much like an AI voice. Um, and second of all, it's American. So I want, you know, that local experience."
- Q7 (P5): "if I was in a car accident and then immediately spoke to an AI assistant, I would be extremely frustrated... It's a stressful environment and AI in its current state, and especially with the, the voice that's been decided to be used in that scenario as very cold in clinical."
- Q8 (P10): "I would've definitely have hung up at the intro. This one? At the intro. Okay. Yeah, it was way too long."

## Theme 4: The introduction is where leaving or escalating is imagined

**Description.** Where participants describe exiting, they place it at the start of the call. Both the human request and the hang up are stated as hypotheticals about what they would do, since the scenarios were prerecorded and no participant is quoted as actually doing either. P5 describes the human request as a first step and hanging up as a fallback.

**Data extracts.**
- Q9 (P4): "If I was gonna do it, that's probably where I would do it" (said about asking for a human at the introduction).
- Q10 (P5): "I would've asked for a human. Yeah. If I didn't get that, then. I probably would've hung up and then tried to do it manually online."
- Q8 (P10), also under Theme 3: "I would've definitely have hung up at the intro."
- Q6 (P1), also under Theme 2: P1 recalls that "if you wanted to, um, get a human. Agent on the line, you could as well at the beginning."

## Written account

The extracts do not support the three hypotheses evenly. They also do not support them as a set. The clearest pattern is that reactions depend on what the AI is compared with. Themes 1 and 3 show the same participants and quotes pulling in opposite directions depending on the reference point. Against clunky chatbots, robotic voices and repeating oneself, the AI is praised (Q1, Q2, Q3). Against what a stressful or local-service moment needs, it is criticised (Q4, Q7, Q8).

H1 (voice AI is more satisfying than traditional voice automation) has partial and indirect support. Three participants speak well of the AI. However, none of the three compares it with traditional voice automation in the sense of a phone menu or interactive voice response system. P1 compares it with website chatbots, P2 with a robotic-sounding voice, and P3 with a repetitive experience that could be of either kind. The material also holds contrary reactions (Q4, Q7) that are not comparisons with automation at all. Satisfaction is therefore suggested for some participants, and the material cannot confirm the comparison the hypothesis states.

H2 (customers do not hang up immediately when they learn they are speaking with an AI) is contradicted for two participants, in stated intention only. P10 says "I would've definitely have hung up at the intro" (Q8) and P5 would hang up if no human were offered (Q10). The reasons given differ in kind. P10 cites the length of the introduction, which is not about the AI disclosure itself. P5's hang up is conditional on being refused a human. No quoted participant says they hung up on learning it was an AI. The material supports "some participants say they would leave early" and does not support or contradict the hypothesis as worded, which is about the moment of learning.

H3 (customers do not immediately ask for a human) is also contradicted for two participants in stated intention. P4 would probably ask at the introduction (Q9) and P5 would ask for a human (Q10). Q5 suggests P4's preference fades as the call proceeds, and P1's annoyance (Q6) was tied to the human option being offered at the start. In this sample, wanting a human appears to be a first reaction that a good process may relieve, which is the reverse of the hypothesis for those participants.

Read together, the introduction is the pivot. Early on, participants imagine asking for a human or hanging up (Q8, Q9, Q10). Later, the same kind of participant reports a good process (Q5). The evidence for H2 and H3 is therefore about the first moments, and the evidence for H1 is about the experience as a whole. These are different points in the call, so they should not be netted off against each other.

For the decision this informs, the safest reading is that the sample does not show customers accepting the AI at the outset. It shows approval that depends on fluency, voice and a short, well-placed introduction, and it shows that a route to a human matters to some participants from the first seconds.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The method assumes the extracts are interpretable in the form given. Only ten quotes from six of eight participants are available, with no surrounding transcript, so each theme rests on one to three extracts. The themes are provisional and neither saturated nor checked against the full set.
- Reflexive analysis normally works from the researcher's complete familiarisation with the data. That phase could only be done on the supplied quotes. Unquoted material, including everything from P8 and P9, could change or contradict these themes.
- The method does not test hypotheses. The verdicts above are interpretive readings of the extracts and not tests.
- No counts are given as findings, in line with the method. Naming which participants said what is not a measure of how common a view is.
- Participants reacted to prerecorded scenarios. Statements about hanging up or asking for a human are reported intentions, not observed behaviour, and the material does not say which of the four scenarios each quote refers to.
- The material does not say how the AI's nature was disclosed, so the "when they learn" wording of H2 cannot be examined directly.

## Alternative explanations

- The positive reactions (Q1, Q2, Q3) and the negative ones (Q4, Q7, Q8) may reflect different scenarios the participants were responding to, not different customer attitudes. Q7 refers to a car accident, which points to the motor claim scenario. The material does not say which scenarios the other quotes concern, so scenario type could explain the split.
- The material is not ordered and the quoted subset is selective. The apparent contrast between enthusiasts and sceptics may follow from which quotes were chosen, not from a real division among the eight participants.
- Individual differences may matter more than the AI's features. P1 appears in both a favourable theme (Q1) and an annoyed one (Q6), which suggests one person can hold both views, and that the "positive" and "negative" groupings are about the quotes rather than about types of customer.
- The wish for a human (Q5, Q9, Q10) may be a general preference for human service in stressful or consequential calls, and not a reaction to this particular AI.
