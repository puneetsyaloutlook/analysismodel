# Toulmin analysis of the adoption measurement article

The article is "Adopt an AI, today: a workaround for measuring AI transformation". The material has no paragraph or section IDs, so each part is referred to by its heading or by quoted text. I identified five arguments that carry the article's point of view and labelled each one: claim (C), ground (G), warrant (W), backing (B), rebuttal (R) and qualifier (Q). Where the author gives no text for a part, the diagram says "not stated" and does not fill the gap with a guess. A warrant marked "implied" is one the ground needs in order to reach the claim, and I say how I derived it.

## Argument 1: organisations measure by adoption because it is countable early

```
G   "Neither shows up quickly in financial results, and few organisations can trace either back to AI specifically."
      (value in the form boards and executives want is slow and hard to attribute)
W   (implied) When the preferred measure is unavailable, organisations report whatever
      can be counted early. The author does not state this.
B   Not stated. The text gives no survey, case or source.
Q   "Adoption seems to have gone mainstream as the way organisations describe their progress with AI"
      ("seems" is the only hedge)
R   Not stated. No organisation that measures differently is mentioned.
C   "So AI programs report adoption instead, because it can be counted from the first week of a rollout."
```

Finding: the ground and the claim are both plausible and consistent with each other. The ground describes one cause (value is slow to show), the claim names that cause as the reason. The unstated warrant is a general rule about organisational behaviour with no backing. The sentence "Following their lead, vendors set their pricing based on seats and usage, and consultancies build their frameworks around it" adds a causal direction (programs lead, vendors follow) that has no ground. The opposite direction, where vendor pricing makes seat and usage data the easiest thing to report, is equally compatible with the text.

## Argument 2: adoption measures miss workarounds

```
G1  "A workaround does not show up as non-use."
G2  "The person still has the licence, still opens the tool and still counts as an active user, so on paper the tool has found a home."
G3  Five examples by level (interface, task, workflow, team, leadership), closed by
      "These are actual observations from research on tools and agentic automation."
W   (stated in part) "If adoption means anything at work, it means the tool works well enough, as designed, for people to get the job done with it. A workaround is its opposite."
      Implied step: a usage measure is therefore inadequate if it cannot tell the two apart.
B   The statement about "actual observations from research" is the only backing, and it names no study, sample or date.
Q   "Faced with any tool at work, a person does one of two things. They use it as designed, or they find a workaround that gets the job done."
      This is an absolute statement. It is not a qualifier, and it removes partial or mixed use from consideration.
R   Not stated. The article does not consider that usage data could be read alongside other signals (support requests, task completion, time per task).
C   Usage-based adoption figures cannot show whether a tool is used as designed or worked around.
```

Finding: this is the strongest argument in the article, because G1 and G2 follow from how usage counting works (a counted log-in does not record what happens afterwards). The weak points are these. First, the examples in G3 show that workarounds can exist, but not how common they are, so the claim that workarounds are a large blind spot has no frequency evidence. Second, the backing is a bare assertion of research. Third, the two-way split in the qualifier position is stated as fact and leaves out use that is partly as designed and partly not. Fourth, the Team example (opening the tool daily so usage is recorded) and the Leadership example (recording enough usage to stay compliant, but not enough to suggest a smaller team) are cases of gaming the metric, which is a different problem from a design workaround. The article groups them under one heading.

## Argument 3: adoption cannot record value that removes the need for work

```
G   Two examples: documents that update automatically so "nobody has to request them, process the request or answer the follow-up call", and a product built so that "the team that checked for that error has nothing left to check."
W   (implied) A usage measure can only register value where AI use increases or where
      a before-and-after comparison of the same work exists.
      The text supports this in part: "nothing in it is being used more than it was before, in fact there is no earlier version of the work to compare it with."
B   Not stated. The examples are hypothetical ("If a customer's documents update...", "If a product is built...").
Q   "some of the most valuable changes" (a partial claim, correctly limited)
R   Not stated.
C   "An adoption measure has no way to record this value."
```

Finding: the argument is valid for usage counts as defined in the article. The ground is hypothetical, so what it shows is that such value is possible, not that it is among the most valuable changes. The claim also cuts against the article's own remedy. The medium-term tier asks the program to "track time, quality, errors, and the experience of staff and customers for each piece of work the program changes, compared with how it was done before", and Argument 3 says removed work has no earlier version to compare. Only the end-state tier ("including work that is no longer needed") addresses it, and that tier is a plan to be tested through small trials, not a measure.

## Argument 4: nobody adopts AI, so adoption is the wrong concept

```
G1  Two cases: a person uses a tool with AI built in, or a person learns a new job because agents now do part of the work.
G2  Analogies: "Nobody describes booking a flight online as adopting ecommerce, or describes a new project management app as adopting the cloud." Web search "followed this path."
W   (implied) If a technology becomes a component of other tools, or if what people do is
      learn a job, then "adopting" that technology is not a real activity, and a program organised around it is mis-specified.
B   The web search history (late 1990s query syntax, now a search box). It is one example and it is given as a pattern, not sourced.
Q   "Taken literally, they do not."  (a qualifier by the word "literally")
R   Not stated. A rebuttal is available: adoption is a normal term for organisation-level
      change (for example, adopting a new system), and the author's own title uses it.
C   "AI adoption assumes that people actually "adopt AI". Taken literally, they do not."
```

Finding: this is the weakest link in the chain, because the warrant depends on a literal reading of a term that practitioners use loosely. The two-case split in G1 is stated as complete ("what people do with those tools falls into two cases") with no argument that other cases do not exist, for example a person who chooses a standalone AI assistant and takes responsibility for its output. That case is exactly the one the article's opening sentence says does not exist ("AI is not a single thing that a person chooses and takes responsibility for"), and the article gives no ground for that. The analogies establish that other technologies lost the label as they became ordinary, but they do not show that AI is on the same path. The sentence "A program built around adopting AI is organised around a technology that is becoming a component of everything" predicts this without evidence. The article's own title and its closing phrase "bringing the AI home" use the language it argues against.

## Argument 5: workarounds show design failure, not resistance

```
G1  "People build workarounds because they are responsible for getting the work done and the tool as designed did not let them do it."
G2  "When an adoption does not work out, the common explanations are that staff are resisting, that leaders are not engaged enough, or that the organisation did not follow the program."
W   (stated) "Each explanation attributes the failure to people. None of them questions the measures the program used or the design of the tools it introduced."
      Implied step: if a failure can be located in design, locating it in people is an error.
B   Not stated. No example is given of a program that blamed people when design was the cause.
Q   "Treating it as resistance discards that information." and "Training people to use the tool as designed will not help when the design is the reason they work around it."
      The second sentence is conditional ("when the design is the reason"), which is a proper limit.
R   Not stated. The article does not allow that some workarounds come from resistance, skill gaps or
      gaming of the metric (its own Team and Leadership examples).
C   "Each workaround shows where the design failed" (the section heading), supported by "A program measured by adoption rates cannot see workarounds, so when the work does not improve it has no evidence about the design and looks for the cause in people instead."
```

Finding: the heading claims every workaround shows a design failure. The article's own Team and Leadership examples (no time for training, a manager shaping usage figures) are failures of workload and incentives, and the remedy it gives for them ("time to learn", "a clear statement of how adoption figures will and will not be used") is not a design change to the tool. The claim therefore needs the qualifier "design in the broad sense, including work organisation", which the article does not state. The list of common explanations in G2 is asserted without a source, and the claim that organisations reach for people-based causes because they lack design evidence is a causal story with no case behind it.

## Argument 6: report value in tiers, starting with workarounds

```
G   "A program still needs early evidence, because the value of AI takes time to show up in the numbers."
W   (implied) Evidence about workarounds is early, collectable and a valid proxy for later value.
      This is not argued. The text says only that "Workarounds found and removed can be reported from the first weeks of a rollout."
B   "Each comes from established practice in building valuable solutions: observe how the work is really done, design for the result rather than the tool, make sure people can do what is asked of them, and start from a clear picture of what is being built."
      No source is given for "established practice".
Q   "it can report value in tiers" ("can", a permissive claim, not a necessity)
R   Not stated. Costs of observing work and interviewing staff are not considered, nor the risk that workaround counts become another target that people manage.
C   An AI program should replace a single usage figure with tiered reporting: short term (tools work as designed), medium term (work is better), longer term (people can do new jobs), end state (the intended future is reached).
```

Finding: the claim follows from Arguments 2 and 5 only if workarounds are common and reliably observed. The warrant that a count of workarounds removed is evidence of value is the unstated step that carries the weight. Fewer workarounds may mean a better tool, or may mean staff have learned not to disclose them, which is the same pattern the article describes for usage figures. The backing is an appeal to what is "obvious" ("One look at that list, and it's obvious: none of these tiers is new"), which is a reason the proposal is low risk, not a reason it works.

## Overall reading of the diagrams

| Argument | Ground quality | Warrant | Likely point of error |
|:-|:-|:-|:-|
| 1 Adoption reported because countable | Plausible, unsourced | Implied, general rule | Causal direction of vendors and programs |
| 2 Adoption misses workarounds | Strong (how counting works) | Partly stated | Frequency of workarounds not shown; backing is a bare assertion of research |
| 3 Adoption misses removed work | Hypothetical examples | Implied | Conflicts with the medium-term tier of the remedy |
| 4 Nobody adopts AI | Analogies | Implied, literal reading | Complete two-case split, and the target term used loosely by practitioners |
| 5 Workarounds show design failure | Assertion | Stated | Heading is wider than the evidence; Team and Leadership examples are not design faults |
| 6 Tiered reporting | General appeal | Implied | Workaround counts treated as a valid early proxy for value |

On the brief's first question, the diagnosis of the phenomenon (adoption is counted, and counts cannot see workarounds or removed work) is supported at the level of logic: the limits of a usage count follow from what it records. The material does not show how widely organisations rely on adoption or how often workarounds occur, so those parts are asserted. On the second question, the point of view is valid where it states what a usage measure cannot show, and weaker where it moves from there to a reason for every failure (Argument 5), to a rejection of the concept (Argument 4), and to a remedy (Argument 6). What it leaves out is evidence of prevalence, any rebuttal, and the possibility that the proposed measures have the same blind spots.

## Assumptions and limitations

- The technique assumes the text is one argument chain that can be split into parts. I split it into six arguments by topic, and a different split (for example one argument with sub-claims) would give different diagrams.
- Warrants marked "implied" are my reconstruction of the step needed to connect ground to claim. The author did not write them, and the author may hold different ones. I tried to keep them to the minimum the ground requires.
- The material is one article with no sources, no date and no publisher, and its text was supplied because the page could not be fetched. The technique can show what the argument rests on, but it cannot check whether the "actual observations from research" exist or whether the examples are typical.
- The technique labels structure. It does not measure how common workarounds are or whether adoption figures are as dominant as claimed. The material cannot answer either.
- Rebuttals are mostly "not stated" because the article contains no counter-argument. The rebuttals I note in Arguments 4 and 5 are my additions and are marked as such.

## Alternative explanations

- The main finding is that the article's diagnosis is logically sound but its prevalence claims and its wider conclusions are unsupported. Another reading is that the article is not trying to prove prevalence. It describes itself as a workaround and draws on research the author has done, so its aim may be to give practitioners a framework to look with, and the missing backing reflects genre (a short opinion piece) and not weak reasoning.
- The gap between the heading of Argument 5 and its examples may be a difference of definition. If "design" covers work organisation, incentives and training time, as it does in the author's WX idea ("which steps people do and which steps are automated, what context passes at each handover"), then the Team and Leadership examples are design failures and the claim holds. The apparent error would then be a definitional choice that the article does not state.
- The tension between Argument 3 and the medium-term tier may be deliberate sequencing. The author may mean that removed work is handled only in the end-state tier, and that the tiers are cumulative, so there is no contradiction.
